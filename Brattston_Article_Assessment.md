# Brattston, "Incense in Ante-Nicene Christianity" — Assessment for Circulation

**Last updated: 260835-49** (created 260835-49; date-stamped, format yymmdd-iteration)

**David W. T. Brattston, *Churchman* 117.3 (2003), pp. 225–233.** Assessed 260835-49 (2026-09-01), against the eight ante-Nicene patristic citations already verified against primary texts at `260835-47`/`260835-48` (`Patristic_Citations_Incense_Verification.md`, `Tertullian_Incense_Passages.md`). Nothing here is a finding about Rev. James.

## Task 1 — Does the article address the counter-evidence? (the question JD needs answered first)

**Partly, and unevenly. He does not hide the two passages — he cites both — but he handles them very differently, and the one he handles badly is the more important one.**

**Tertullian burning frankincense himself, *De Corona* 10 — handled honestly.** Brattston's footnote 11 reads: *"In De corona he conceded that animals can be killed and incense burned for secular purposes but not for worship,"* and he glosses this with a modern analogy (deodorizer sprays). That is an accurate summary of what chapter 10 actually says — Tertullian's own example is himself burning frankincense at home to clear a bad smell, expressly distinguished from the "ceremony... dress... pomp" of idol-sacrifice. Brattston doesn't quote the sentence itself and doesn't flag that this is Tertullian's own domestic practice rather than a hypothetical, but the substance — a conceded secular/non-worship use — is fairly reported, and lands in the same place the primary-text verification did.

**Christians buying incense for funerals, *Apology* 42 — handled evasively.** Brattston's footnote 9: *"His Apologeticus denounces buying incense as wasteful, saying that the Christian practice is to spend money on burying their dead instead of 'the fumigating of the gods.'"* The actual text (quoted in full at `Tertullian_Incense_Passages.md` §5) says something importantly different: *"We certainly buy no frankincense. If the Arabias complain of this, let the Sabaeans be well assured that their more precious and costly merchandise is expended as largely in the burying of Christians as in the fumigating of the gods."* Tertullian's point is commercial reassurance to the incense trade — Christians spend **just as much** Sabaean incense on their own funerals as pagans spend fumigating idols. Christians were buying incense, in bulk, for a non-idolatrous purpose. Brattston's "instead of" phrasing erases that: a reader of the article would come away thinking Christians substituted burial expenses for incense expenses, not that the burial expenses *were* incense expenses. This is the single passage `260835-47` calls "the citation that most clearly cuts against the argument it is deployed for," and it is the one place in the article where the primary text is softened rather than reported.

**A third relevant passage — not from the eight, and handled well.** Footnote 12 quotes *De Idololatria* 11 directly: incense is licit "for medicinal ointments" and "solaces of sepulture" (funerals), even while Tertullian forbids the trade of *selling* incense for pagan sacrifice specifically. This is the passage `260835-48` calls the single most systematic Tertullian statement on the whole question, and Brattston quotes its actual carve-out language rather than paraphrasing around it. Good, direct handling — better than his handling of *Apology* 42, on the very same point.

**Other non-liturgical/non-sacrificial Christian incense use in the ante-Nicene period:** the article does not mention the Magi's frankincense offered to the infant Christ (*De Idololatria* 9), a passage `260835-48` found genuinely double-edged and outside the article's own eight-citation core. Not an omission that changes anything by itself — it's ambiguous evidence either way — but it means the article's coverage of "Christian-adjacent, non-sacrificial incense" is not exhaustive even within Tertullian's own corpus.

**The bottom line for Task 1:** Brattston does not conceal the existence of secular/domestic and funerary Christian incense use — he cites both categories, with real quotations in two of the three instances. But the specific sentence most likely to embarrass the article's thesis if a reader opens the primary text — Christians buying Sabaean incense in quantity for their own funerals — is the one sentence he paraphrases in a way that obscures rather than states it. That is worth knowing before circulating the piece, and it is a narrower, more specific problem than "he omitted the counter-evidence."

## Task 2 — Legal-historical claims

**No claim in this article carries the specific defect `260835-44` found in the project's own 1559 claim** (an unqualified "outlawed" where no instrument names the practice). Brattston's closest analogues are two dating disputes, both explicitly hedged:

- **The Peter of Alexandria funeral (A.D. 311), footnotes 52–54.** Brattston reports that some liturgical historians (Atchley, Marsh) date the first known instance of Christian incense use to this event, then immediately undercuts it: the surviving records "were compiled in their present form only in the seventh century [and] are quite unreliable for a detail of this kind," quoting Gregory Dix, *The Shape of the Liturgy* (1945), p. 427 n.5. He does the same for the Constantine-donation legend, tracing it to the sixth-century *Liber Pontificalis*. This is the opposite failure mode from the 1559 claim: a real dating problem is stated as a real dating problem, with a named source and page number, not smoothed into a confident assertion.
- **The Apostolic Canons, footnotes 55–59.** Brattston flags an active scholarly dispute — earlier editors (Donaldson, Percival) allowing an ante-Nicene date, later ones (Dragas, Connolly, Easton) placing the material in the late fourth century — and argues from internal contradiction with Hippolytus's *Apostolic Tradition* rather than resting on the date alone. Spot check: New Advent's own header for the Apostolic Canons (reached via *Apostolic Constitutions* Book VIII, ch. 47) dates the material "(c. 400)," consistent with the later-dating side Brattston favors. The dispute is real and Brattston represents both camps by name rather than picking one silently.

**Conclusion for Task 2:** no comparable unsupported legal-historical claim was found. Where Brattston's argument depends on a contested date, he says so, names the disputants, and generally takes the more cautious reading rather than the one that would help his thesis most (e.g., he does not lean on the earlier ante-Nicene dating of the Apostolic Canons, which would have strengthened his case).

## Task 3 — Overall framing: does the article overstate uniformity?

**Yes, and it is the same conflation `260835-47` found underneath the source table this article's citations were drawn from.**

Brattston's conclusion states flatly that "opposition to incense in worship was the position of the one, holy, catholic and apostolic church," built on eight ante-Nicene citations plus an argument from the silence of the Didache and Hippolytus's *Apostolic Tradition*. The word doing the work is **"worship."** The primary-text verification of all eight citations found that every one of the six theological arguments among them (Justin ×2, Athenagoras, Barnabas, Tertullian *Apology* 30, and — with the fourth-century caveat — Lactantius) is aimed specifically at incense **offered as sacrificial oblation**, in a contrast class that is always a victim on an altar: blood, libations, burnt-offerings, a hecatomb, the fat of lambs. **Not one of the eight, read in full, addresses incense used ceremonially or honorifically within a Christian assembly, directed to God rather than to an idol or in place of pagan state sacrifice.** That is a real and specific gap, not a technicality: a ritualist defender of the thurible does not claim incense is a propitiatory sacrifice or that God needs to be fed by it, so a Father's denial that God needs sacrificial smoke does not, by itself, reach the different claim that incense may not be burned honorifically in worship.

Brattston's article never argues for the move from "condemned as pagan-style sacrificial oblation" to "condemned in any Christian liturgical use whatsoever" — it treats the two as obviously the same claim, which is exactly the equation `260835-47` found the primary texts do not support. The argument-from-silence section (Didache, Pliny, Justin's summary in *1 Apology* 61 and 65–67, and especially Hippolytus's *Apostolic Tradition*, which itemizes exorcism-breath, oil, water, milk and honey, specific fruits, flowers, cheese and olives but never incense) is a genuinely different and independently defensible line of argument — silence in an exhaustive ritual inventory carries real evidentiary weight — and it is **not** addressed by the `260835-47`/`260835-48` verification, which was scoped to the eight named citations. It is worth noting as the article's strongest single point, and as something this pass has not itself checked against the primary texts of the Didache or the *Apostolic Tradition*.

One more point in the article's favor on framing: Brattston is honest about the Gnostic exception (the *Second Book of Jeu*), correctly identifies it as Gnostic rather than mainstream, and explains in a paragraph why it carries negligible weight rather than quietly omitting it. That is the same kind of disclosure this section is faulting him for skipping elsewhere — so the problem is not a general pattern of concealment, it is specifically the slide from "sacrificial oblation" to "any liturgical use" in the conclusion, plus the one paraphrase flagged in Task 1.

## Task 4 — Light citation spot-check (four citations, none among the eight already verified)

All four checked against New Advent's Ante-Nicene/Nicene Fathers text (Tier 1, single-sourced — not double-attested, consistent with this being a light sanity check rather than a full audit).

| Citation | Claim | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Constantine, *Oratio ad sanctorum coetum* 12 (footnote 7) | Martyr-commemoration "sacrifice of thanksgiving... wherein is no need of the fragrant frankincense, no need of fire" | **Confirmed, verbatim.** Found at newadvent.org/fathers/2503.htm, ch. 12, word-for-word as quoted. Context (a bloodless offering of hymns and light in memory of martyrs) matches Brattston's use of it as a "passing comment," not an express condemnation — correctly filed under that heading. |
| Arnobius, *Adversus Gentes* 6.1 (footnote 15) | "do not build altars, do not offer the blood of creatures slain in sacrifices, incense, nor sacrificial meal" | **Confirmed, verbatim**, opening sentence of Book VI, newadvent.org/fathers/06316.htm. |
| Arnobius, *Adversus Gentes* 7.26 (footnote 18) | Incense is "almost a novelty," unknown to "the ancient writers, in whose books is found no mention of it" | **Confirmed, verbatim**, ch. 26, newadvent.org/fathers/06317.htm. |
| Arnobius, *Adversus Gentes* 7.28 (footnote 17) | "a thousand pounds of the finest incense, and the whole sky clouded with the darkness of abundant vapours" | **Confirmed, verbatim**, ch. 28, same page. |

**Result: 4 of 4 confirmed as claimed, no distortion found in any of the four.** This is a better spot-check result than the *Apology* 42 case in Task 1 — these four are all report-only citations (Brattston is quoting what an author says, not compressing an argument), and report-only citations are where this article is most reliable. The one real problem found in this whole assessment (*Apology* 42) is a paraphrase of an argument, not a misquotation.

## Verdict

**Safe to circulate with one caveat you should be ready to supply yourself if pressed: the article's account of *Apology* 42 is the passage to know cold before you send this anywhere.** If a ritualist reader opens Tertullian's actual text, the "we buy no frankincense" line reverses in the very next clause — Christians bought Sabaean incense in bulk for their own funerals — and Brattston's paraphrase doesn't prepare a reader for that. Everything else checked in this pass (four spot-checked citations, the two legal-historical dating disputes, the *De Corona* 10 and *De Idololatria* 11 handling) holds up as accurate and, in the *De Idololatria* case, better than adequate.

The larger issue is the one in Task 3: the article's title and conclusion promise more than its own evidence delivers. It is a genuinely strong, well-sourced case that the ante-Nicene Fathers rejected incense as sacrificial oblation, offered while refusing pagan or Jewish cult — and a considerably weaker, essentially silent, case on whether any of them addressed incense burned honorifically within Christian worship itself. Circulating it as "the ante-Nicene evidence against incense" is fine; circulating it as settling the ceremonial question specifically will draw exactly the reply the primary texts make available.

**Recommendation:** safe to circulate with a caveat — either your own cover note flagging the *Apology* 42 point, or simply being ready to concede it if raised, since conceding it does not cost the article's real argument (the sacrificial-oblation point, which is solid). Not "not recommended" — the article is honest in the aggregate and the errors found are narrow and specific, not a pattern of misrepresentation.
