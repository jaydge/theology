# Calvin, Luther, and Anglican Formularies on Iconography

Fact-check of claims made by RJ (Craig Truglia's interlocutor) in the "Anglican and Orthodox Dialogue, with Craig Truglia" video (timestamp 31:57–32:39, [youtube.com/watch?v=q7XR5CJrzM4](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q7XR5CJrzM4)).

## The claims under review

RJ states that Calvin "was not opposed to iconography as sort of iconography itself being idolatrous," that he treated the abuse as a prudential problem which could be revisited "in later generations," that "Lutherans have never really had an issue with iconography," and that "most Anglicans" he speaks with — including Fr. Matt Kennedy's parish — have no problem with icons.

## Calvin

Calvin does draw a real distinction between religious art in general and cultic images. He is explicit that art itself is not the problem: "I am not, however, so superstitious as to think that all visible representations of every kind are unlawful. But as sculpture and painting are gifts of God..." (*Institutes* 1.11.12). To that extent RJ's summary has a basis — Calvin is not a blanket iconoclast about painting and sculpture as art forms.

But two further points in the same chapter undercut RJ's specific claims that Calvin's objection was merely prudential and reversible.

**On whether the danger is permanent, not merely a passing abuse:** immediately after arguing that the church went five centuries without images in worship because the fathers judged them dangerous on principle, Calvin writes:

> "we know too well from experience that the moment images appear in churches, idolatry has as it were raised its banner; because the folly of manhood cannot moderate itself, but forthwith falls away to superstitious worship." (*Institutes* 1.11.13)

This is framed as a general claim about human nature ("experience," "the folly of manhood"), not a claim limited to a particular historical abuse that later generations might outgrow. It echoes the more famous line earlier in the same chapter that "man's nature... is a perpetual factory of idols" (*idolorum fabrica*, 1.11.8).

Calvin then adds a second, independent argument that doesn't depend on abuse at all:

> "Even were the danger less imminent, still, when I consider the proper end for which churches are erected, it appears to me more unbecoming their sacredness than I well can tell, to admit any other images than those living symbols which the Lord has consecrated by his own word: I mean Baptism and the Lord's Supper..." (1.11.13)

Even setting aside the risk of idolatry entirely, Calvin holds that only the sacraments count as God-authorized "images" in the church. This is a standing theological judgment, not a temporary quarantine pending a future, less-abusive era — so RJ's "bring it back later" reading has no clear textual support in Calvin, and is arguably contradicted by it.

**On devotional use short of formal "worship" (the prayer-corner/candle case):** Calvin approvingly cites Augustine for exactly this scenario — images used devotionally, independent of any explicit doctrine of veneration:

> "When images are thus placed aloft in seats of honour, to be beheld by those who are praying or sacrificing, though they have neither sense nor life, yet from appearing as if they had both, they affect weak minds just as if they lived and breathed." (Augustine, *Ep.* 49, quoted at 1.11.13)

Calvin's point is that devotional placement alone — without a formal theology of worship — already corrupts the practice. So the art/cultic-use distinction is real and is Calvin's own (he explicitly allows "historical" and secular pictorial art at 1.11.12), but he denies it survives once an image functions as an object of devotion in a worship space, which is precisely the icon-corner/candle scenario.

## Luther and the Lutherans

Luther, against Karlstadt's iconoclasm, defended images as adiaphora — neither commanded nor forbidden, permissible as long as they are not worshipped: "A crucifix, or any other holy image, is not forbidden" (*Against the Heavenly Prophets in the Matter of Images and Sacraments*, 1525). He treated the real work of reform as internal ("I approached the task of destroying images by first tearing them out of the heart through God's Word"), not the removal of physical images.

This supports "Lutherans never smashed all images," but it is not the same claim as "Lutherans have no problem with iconography" in the Orthodox sense. Luther's doctrine grants images zero religious efficacy and explicitly forbids venerating them — nothing resembling the proskynesis (veneration) mandated by the Second Council of Nicaea (787). The Formula of Concord's adiaphora framework treats such ceremonies/objects as "no worship of God, nor any part of it" in themselves. So "no issue with iconography" is true only in the narrow sense of tolerating didactic art; it overstates the position if taken to mean acceptance of icon veneration.

## Anglican formularies

Article XXII of the Thirty-Nine Articles (1571) rejects "the Romish Doctrine concerning... worshipping and adoration, as well of Images as of Reliques." Read in isolation, this is narrow — it condemns venerating images, not possessing them — and RJ's reading of the bare Article text is defensible on its own terms.

However, Article XXXV commends the Books of Homilies as containing "godly and wholesome Doctrine," and the homily "An Homily Against Peril of Idolatry and Superfluous Decking of Churches" is the official exposition of Article XXII's rationale. It explicitly denies that a presence/worship distinction can be maintained in practice:

> "our Images also have been, and be, and if they be publicly suffered in Churches and Temples, ever will be also worshipped, and so Idolatry committed to them."

The homily also denies that "idol" and "image" pick out different things in a religious context, calling that distinction a device that differs "only in sound and language" and exists to blind people to the danger. On this basis it argues images "ought to be taken out of the churches" altogether, not merely stripped of formal veneration.

So RJ can fairly claim the Article's text alone permits his reading (images allowed, provided not intended for worship). He cannot fairly claim that reading represents the historic Church of England's own reasoning: the document that explains the Article rejects the premise that presence without formal veneration is stable or containable.

**On contemporary Anglican practice:** confessional/Reformed-wing Anglicanism (GAFCON-aligned provinces, Sydney Anglicans, the Reformed Episcopal Church, much of the Global South) still formally subscribes to the Articles and Homilies and would reject icon veneration on these grounds. RJ's observation that "most Anglicans I speak with" and "every parish I've visited" have icons likely reflects a real but sociologically narrow slice — Anglo-Catholic and Anglo-Catholic-adjacent circles — not Anglicanism as a whole, whose numerical center is now in Africa and includes many low-church provinces.

**On Fr. Matt Kennedy specifically:** his parish (Church of the Good Shepherd, Binghamton, NY — ACNA, self-described "traditional Reformed Anglican") was confirmed to exist, but no public source was found confirming or denying whether the parish displays icons. RJ's specific claim about that parish remains unverified from public information and should be checked directly (e.g., by asking Fr. Kennedy or viewing parish photos) rather than taken as established.

## Bottom-line verdict

RJ is right that Calvin, Luther, and even the Thirty-Nine Articles distinguish religious art from formal image-worship, and that none of them treats painted wood and canvas as automatically, metaphysically idolatrous apart from use. He overstates this into something close to indifference toward iconography as a category. Calvin's own text frames the danger as rooted in a permanent feature of human nature (the "idol factory" heart) rather than a passing, reversible abuse, and gives an independent, abuse-independent reason to exclude images from churches. Luther's adiaquora tolerance never extended to veneration. And the Homily that authoritatively glosses Article XXII explicitly rejects the idea that images can be safely present in churches without eventually being worshipped — which is the crux of RJ's claimed distinction.
