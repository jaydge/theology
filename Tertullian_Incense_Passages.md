## 2. Task 1 — *De Corona* 11: the soldier line

**Exact reference:** *De Corona*, chapter 11 (Latin *De Corona Militis*, cap. XI).
Both hosts number it identically; CCEL's URL path (`anf03.iv.vi.xi.html`) and its
"Chapter XI." running head independently confirm the same chapter division as New
Advent's `## Chapter 11`.

**The line, in full context** (both hosts, word-for-word):

> "And shall the Christian be burned according to camp rule, when he was **not
> permitted to burn incense to an idol**, when to him Christ remitted the punishment
> of fire?"

This sits inside a long chain of rhetorical questions — Tertullian's real subject in
ch. 11 is **whether military service itself is lawful for a Christian at all**, not
incense. The incense clause is one item in a list of things a Christian soldier
cannot do without compromise: swear a second oath after Christ's, carry a weapon,
stand guard at temples he has renounced, carry a flag "hostile to Christ." The
incense clause is invoked as an *already-settled premise* ("he was not permitted") —
Tertullian is not arguing for the rule here, he is using it as one plank of a
different argument.

**Register: PRUDENTIAL, narrowly.** This confirms `260835-47`'s classification
independently rather than assuming it. The reason given is not a positive claim
about what God is or wants (that is the theological register found at *Apology* 30
and in Justin/Athenagoras); it is a rule of avoidance — don't perform an act of
formal idol-worship — cited as a given, alongside other avoidance-rules that are
themselves prudential (don't swear the military oath, don't guard the temple). The
underlying *reason* the rule exists is theological (idolatry is a first-order
offense against God), but the *register of this passage's argument* is prudential:
it lists what a Christian must decline, without arguing the nature of true worship.
That distinction is why this project's own taxonomy (six theological, one
prudential, among the eight) calls this the one prudential citation.

**Safe to deploy?** Yes, narrowly, and only at the correct chapter. It supports:
*"Tertullian treats burning incense to an idol as forbidden, on a level with the
army's other idolatrous observances."* It does **not** support any claim about
Christian liturgical incense, which this passage never addresses — the object of
the ban is an idol, not incense as a substance or a practice.

## 3. Task 2 — *De Corona* 10: Tertullian burning frankincense himself

**Full context of the relevant passage** (both hosts, word-for-word):

> "The substances are themselves as creatures of God without impurity, and in this
> their native state are free to the use of all; but the ministries to which in
> their use they are devoted, makes all the difference; for I, too, kill a cock for
> myself, just as Socrates did for Æsculapius; and **if the smell of some place or
> other offends me, I burn the Arabian product myself**, but not with the same
> ceremony, nor in the same dress, nor with the same pomp, with which it is done to
> idols."

This is the chapter's core argument, and it is not about incense — it is about
**crowns**. Chapter 10 answers the objection that pagan deities are "crowned," by
arguing that a crown is not defiled by being a physical object (a "creature of God")
but by the cultic *use* to which it is put. Tertullian reaches for incense as his
own illustration of that use/substance distinction, and the illustration is himself:
he burns "the Arabian product" (frankincense) in his own house, as a deodorizer,
whenever a bad smell offends him — a mundane, domestic, non-cultic use, explicitly
contrasted with "the same ceremony... dress... pomp" of idol-sacrifice.

**Register:** this is not a prohibition and has no register on the theological/
prudential axis used elsewhere in this file — it is a **permission**, stated in
Tertullian's own first person, and it undercuts rather than supports any argument
that the early Fathers regarded incense as inherently idolatrous or theologically
condemned as a substance. CCEL's ANF editorial footnote at this exact sentence reads:
*"He seems to know no use for incense except for burials and for fumigation"* —
recorded here as the editor's own summary judgment, not as Tertullian's text, but it
independently corroborates the reading above.

**Does this help or hurt the argument that the Fathers condemned incense
theologically? It hurts it, plainly, and is reported that way.** Tertullian's actual
position in this chapter is precisely the opposite of a blanket condemnation: the
object is neutral, the rite is what defiles. **This chapter must never be cited as
Tertullian condemning incense** — the only defensible citation from it is the
crown-and-idolatry argument it is actually making, with the frankincense sentence
disclosed as counter-evidence if incense comes up at all.

## 4. Task 3 — *Apology* 30: explicit verdict

**What it says about incense:** Tertullian describes the sacrifice Christians
actually offer for the emperor — "that costly and noble sacrifice of prayer... not
the few grains of incense a farthing buys — tears of an Arabian tree — not a few
drops of wine, — not the blood of some worthless ox." The passage is not describing
what Christians *withhold* from Caesar; it describes what Christians offer *to God*
on Caesar's behalf, contrasted with the cheap, mass-produced materials of pagan
state sacrifice (a farthing's worth of incense, a few drops of wine, a sacrificial
ox).

**Register: THEOLOGICAL.** The reason is stated directly — this is the sacrifice
God Himself requires ("at His own requirement"), from "a chaste body, an unstained
soul, a sanctified spirit." CCEL's editorial footnote at this sentence: *"Once more
this reflection on the use of material incense, which is common to early
Christians, as in former volumes noted"* — again, editorial opinion, recorded as
such, but confirming this is a recognized recurring Tertullianic trope rather than a
singular remark.

**Verdict: safe to deploy WITH CAVEAT, not without one.** The caveat is the same one
`Patristic_Citations_Incense_Verification.md` §3 already raised as the governing
scope problem for all eight citations: the contrast class here is *pagan sacrificial
incense offered as state oblation*, not incense used ceremonially within Christian
worship. Tertullian is devaluing the material offering relative to the spiritual one
**in the specific context of what is or isn't owed to the emperor's cult** — he is
not addressing, and this passage cannot be stretched to address, whether incense
used liturgically within a Christian assembly, directed to God rather than Caesar or
an idol, would trouble him one way or the other. Cited for "Tertullian regarded
cultic/imperial incense-offering as spiritually worthless next to the sacrifice of
prayer" — sound. Cited for "Tertullian rejected incense as such" — overreach the
text does not support.

## 5. Task 4 — *Apology* 42: the funerary counter-evidence

**What it says, in full context** (both hosts, word-for-word):

> "We do not buy a crown for my head... We **certainly buy no frankincense**. If the
> Arabias complain of this, let the Sabæans be well assured that their more precious
> and costly merchandise **is expended as largely in the burying of Christians** as
> in the fumigating of the gods."

This is inside a chapter answering the charge that Christians are economically
useless — they use the forum, the baths, the markets, pay their taxes, and so on.
The incense line is commercial bookkeeping, not theology: temple revenue is down,
Tertullian says, but the Sabaean incense-traders lose nothing, because Christians
spend just as much buying incense for funerals as pagans spend burning it to their
gods.

**Is funerary use distinguished from liturgical use, or the same category?**
Distinguished — but not from *liturgical* use specifically. The distinction drawn in
the text is between **cultic/idolatrous use** ("fumigating of the gods") and
**funerary use** ("the burying of Christians"). Christian liturgical incense — used
in Christian worship, directed to God — is a *third* category this passage never
raises at all. It cannot be read as evidence either for or against that third
category; it only establishes that Tertullian regarded buying substantial
quantities of frankincense, for a non-idolatrous purpose, as unremarkable and worth
mentioning almost with pride ("let the Sabæans be well assured").

**Can this passage be cited for anything, or must it be dropped entirely?** The
opening five words alone — *"We certainly buy no frankincense"* — **must never be
quoted in isolation.** Read that way, it says the reverse of what the chapter as a
whole says, and it is disproven by Tertullian's own very next clause. ⛔ This
matches `260835-47`'s own finding at §3 and is independently confirmed here rather
than merely repeated. **If deployed at all, it must carry the full sentence** — the
qualification is not a footnote to the claim, it is the claim. Used honestly, it is
legitimate evidence that ordinary Christians bought incense in bulk for funerals
without any sense of impropriety, which is worth having on record precisely because
it is counter-evidence to an unqualified "the Fathers rejected incense" reading —
but it says nothing about worship.

## 6. Task 5 — other Tertullian incense passages

### 5a. *De Idololatria*, chapter 2 — definitional list

Tertullian, defining idolatry's "more limited sense" before arguing for a much wider
one, lists the paradigm acts most people (wrongly, in his view) think idolatry is
limited to: *"if one burn incense, or immolate a victim, or give a sacrificial
banquet, or be bound to some sacred functions or priesthoods."* This is not an
argument about incense — incense is one item in a four-item list illustrating a
narrow, inadequate definition Tertullian is about to argue past (he goes on to
define idolatry as extending to covetousness, false witness, and other things far
removed from cult objects). **Usable only as evidence that burning incense to an
idol was the paradigm case of formal idol-worship in Tertullian's mental furniture**
— not as an argument in itself.

### 5b. *De Idololatria*, chapter 9 — the Magi's frankincense to Christ

New finding, not in `260835-47`'s eight. Discussing astrology (the chapter's actual
subject), Tertullian notes that the Magi *"offered to the then infant Lord that
frankincense and myrrh and gold, to be, as it were, **the close of worldly sacrifice
and glory, which Christ was about to do away**."*

⚠️⚠️ **This is genuinely double-edged and is reported as such rather than resolved
in either direction.** On one reading, Tertullian treats a literal, physical
frankincense offering made *to Christ himself* (not an idol) as fitting and
unobjectionable in the moment it happened — evidence against any blanket discomfort
with incense directed at the true object of worship. On the other reading, the
phrase "the close of... which Christ was about to do away" frames that very offering
as the **terminus** of an entire mode of worship — literal sacrificial gift-giving —
that Christ's coming supersedes, which could be read to support an argument that
Tertullian regarded material offerings (incense included) as belonging to a
superseded economy. The passage does not resolve which reading Tertullian intended;
it is three sentences inside a chapter about astrology, not a developed statement
about worship. ⛔ **Do not deploy this one-sided.** If used, both readings must be
disclosed together.

### 5c. *De Idololatria*, chapter 11 — the frankincense-seller's trade

⭐⭐⭐ **New finding, not in `260835-47`'s eight, and the single most systematic
statement on incense anywhere in the Tertullian material this project has now
examined.** The chapter's actual subject is which trades a Christian convert must
give up. On incense specifically, in one continuous passage, Tertullian states
**three distinct rulings**:

1. **Forbidden:** a Christian may not remain in the trade of a *frankincense-seller*
   (thurarius) who supplies incense for pagan sacrifice — "the frankincense-seller is
   a something even more serviceable even toward demons... idolatry is more easily
   carried on without the idol, than without the ware of the frankincense-seller."
   The reasoning is explicitly theological: material complicity in enabling
   idol-worship, not the substance itself.
2. **Permitted:** frankincense used "for medicinal ointments."
3. **Permitted:** frankincense used by Christians "for solaces of sepulture" —
   funerary use, matching *Apology* 42 exactly.

The governing sentence, quoted in full because the three-way distinction is the
whole point: *"If the selfsame merchandises — frankincense, I mean, and all other
foreign productions — used as sacrifice to idols, are of use likewise to men for
medicinal ointments, to us Christians also, over and above, for solaces of
sepulture, let them see to it."*

**Register: theological, but explicitly and narrowly bounded** — the target is
complicity in supplying a specific idolatrous trade, not incense as a substance.
Tertullian goes out of his way, in the same breath as the prohibition, to name two
licit uses. **This is the strongest available Tertullian passage for a claim like
"Tertullian forbade Christian participation in supplying incense for pagan
sacrifice"** — a real, theologically-grounded, non-miscited claim — **and it is
simultaneously the clearest primary-text evidence that Tertullian did not regard
incense itself, or its ordinary domestic and funerary uses, as objectionable.** Any
deployment of this passage that quotes the prohibition without the medicinal/funerary
carve-out in the same sentence would misrepresent it exactly as *Apology* 42's
opening clause would if quoted alone.

⚠️ **Not double-attested — New Advent only.** Flagged in §0 as owed work before
outward deployment.

### 5d. *Ad Scapulam* — checked, empty

Read in full (5 chapters, ANF/Thelwall, New Advent). **No occurrence of "incense" or
"frankincense" anywhere in the text.** This is a short open letter to the proconsul
of Africa warning against renewed persecution; its subject matter (toleration,
divine judgments on persecutors, examples of plagues and deaths) does not touch
sacrifice or cult practice in a way that invokes incense. Reported as a negative
finding, not skipped.

### 5e. *De Oratione* — checked, empty of the word; one adjacent parallel

Read in full (29 chapters, ANF/Thelwall, New Advent). **No occurrence of "incense,"
"frankincense," "odour/odor," or "perfume" anywhere in the text** — including no
citation of Psalm 141:2 ("let my prayer be set forth before thee as incense"),
which would have been the natural place for it in a treatise on prayer.

One adjacent, non-incense passage is worth recording because it is thematically
close to *Apology* 30's argument: chapter 28, "Of the Spiritual Victim, Which Prayer
Is," argues (citing 1 Peter 2:5 and John 4:23–24) that prayer offered "in spirit" is
"the spiritual victim which has abolished the pristine sacrifices," quoting the
Isaiah/Hosea-type rejection of animal sacrifice ("I am full of holocausts of
rams... I desire not the fat of rams"). This is the same "spiritual sacrifice
supersedes literal offering" move found at *Apology* 30, but **it never mentions
incense, frankincense, or any material offering by name** — it is about blood
sacrifice generally. ⛔ **This passage must not be cited as an incense passage.** It
is recorded here only because a future pass reconstructing Tertullian's "spiritual
sacrifice" theme across his corpus should know it exists and know exactly what it
does and does not say.
