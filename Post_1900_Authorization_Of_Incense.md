# Post-1900 Authorization of Liturgical Incense

**Stamp: 260835-60** (ASSIGNED, not derived — two other passes were
running concurrently; the stamp registry was deliberately not read.)

**Mode: APPEND.** This pass ran against a READ-ONLY repository. Nothing
in `~/EMC/theology` was created, edited, moved or deleted; no git write
command was run; the only git commands used were `--no-optional-locks`
reads. Repo HEAD observed at start and end: `cb833ea`. All output is in
`~/EMC/staging-60`.

**Question put to this pass.** JD's public claim has been that
liturgical incense was never positively authorized in either the Church
of England or the American Episcopal Church before 1900. The 1900 cutoff
was chosen for convenience. Does the claim extend past it — and if it
breaks, exactly where and on what instrument?

**The orchestration thread's working belief, which this pass was told to
test rather than confirm**, was that neither church has ever positively
authorized incense by name, and that what changed was the regime, from
litigated lawfulness to presumed discretion. That belief survives the
test for the Church of England. **It does not survive intact for the
American church.** See §3 and the summary table.

---

## §1 — The distinction applied throughout

Three categories, applied to every finding in §2, §3 and §5.

**POSITIVE AUTHORIZATION** — an instrument that grants or permits
liturgical incense **by name**. The instrument must do the permitting,
and incense must be what is permitted.

**PERMISSION BY SILENCE** — no prohibition, with discretion vested
elsewhere. The instrument is silent on incense; something outside it
does the permitting, and does so for a class of acts of which incense is
merely one instance.

**MENTION WITHOUT GRANT** — a rubric, note, form or directory that
**names** incense without authorizing it. This is the category that does
the most work in what follows, and the one most easily misreported. A
text that says "if incense is used, it is appropriate at this point" is
regulating the placement of a practice it takes as given from outside
itself. It is not a grant. It is also not nothing, and §3 records
honestly how close the accumulation of such mentions comes.

**A fourth thing worth naming, because it recurs:** a text may be
*commended* rather than *authorized*. Commended material is not an
instrument of grant at all; it is a suggestion as to how a discretion
conferred elsewhere might be exercised. §2(f) turns on this.

**VERDICT §1: VERIFIED** — the distinction is stated and is applied to
every finding below. Each finding carries its classification on its
face.

## §2 — Church of England

### 2(0) Was the 1899 Opinion ever formally reversed, superseded, or set aside?

**Nothing was found that reversed it.** No Measure, Canon, court
judgment, synodical act or later archiepiscopal opinion naming the
Opinion of 31 July 1899 and setting it aside surfaced in this pass. That
is recorded as an absence with a search record, not as a proof of
non-existence — the query strings and URLs are logged in
`SRC_PRIMARY_1928-2000_ChurchOfEngland_ServiceBooks_Incense-Search.txt`.

What *was* established is that the Opinion was never a court judgment to
begin with, so "reversal" is the wrong instrument to look for. The
Opinion grounds itself in the Prayer Book's own reference procedure, not
in jurisdiction: the Book "requires all persons … to resort to the
Bishop of the Diocese, who may, if he think fit, send the question to
the Archbishop for his decision … We entreat the Clergy … to accept our
decision." Within a year the Earl of Portsmouth told the Lords of the
Lambeth hearing, "it is not a legal tribunal" (HL Deb 16 July 1900 vol
86 cc10-36). This dovetails exactly with what the repo already holds
from the 1906 Royal Commission: the ceremonial use of incense had been
held illegal **in the Court of Arches only**, and "THE QUESTION HAS
NEVER BEEN BEFORE THE JUDICIAL COMMITTEE"
(`src/SRC_PRIMARY_1906_RoyalCommission_Ecclesiastical-Discipline-Incense.txt`).

What happened instead of reversal was **structural removal of the ground
the Opinion stood on**. The Opinion's reasoning runs on the Act of 1559
— its "none other or otherwise" prohibition, and the Crown-with-the-
Archbishop power the Opinion names as the only route to any future
authorization ("even now the liturgical use of incense is not by law
permanently excluded"). Schedule 2 to the 1974 Measure repealed the Act
of Uniformity 1558 outright. See 2(d).

**Classification: PERMISSION BY SILENCE** — nothing named incense to
reverse anything about it. **Verdict: PARTIAL.** The negative (no
reversing instrument found) is solid; the positive account of *why* no
reversal exists rests on the Opinion's self-description plus one Hansard
line, which is good but is not the same as an authoritative modern
ruling on the Opinion's status.

### 2(a) The 1906 Royal Commission on Ecclesiastical Discipline

Read from the existing repo capture before searching afresh, as
instructed. It **names incense** repeatedly, and it is squarely a report
*about* incense — but it is a Command Paper reporting on breaches of the
law, not an instrument granting anything. Its own account of the state
of the law is the opposite of a grant: "IT HAS BEEN HELD IN THE COURT OF
ARCHES THAT THE CEREMONIAL USE OF INCENSE IS ILLEGAL." It also records
the bishops' 1901 joint letter upholding "the duty of submitting to the
decision of the Archbishops."

**Classification: MENTION WITHOUT GRANT.** **Verdict: VERIFIED.**

### 2(b) The 1927 and 1928 revision attempts

**The Deposited Book does not name incense.** A sweep of the full
transcription (thirteen text pages plus preface and exhortation) for
*incense / censer / thurible / thurifer / frankincense* returned exactly
one hit, and it is Scripture — an Epiphany opening Sentence in the new
Introduction to Morning or Evening Prayer, quoting Malachi 1:11 (the
transcription mis-sets the reference as Matthew). The alternative
Communion order, including its General Rubricks on vesture, is clean.

**It also acquired no authorization.** Its own front matter records the
Commons resolution "defeated on 15th December, 1927" and the second
"defeated in the House of Commons on 14th June, 1928", and states in
bold: "The publication of this Book does not directly or indirectly
imply that it can be regarded as authorized for use in churches."

⛔ **UNMATCHED:** the 1929 bishops' declaration permitting the Deposited
Book's use. Only a secondary sentence was obtained, and the phrase
"during the present emergency" must **not** be deployed as a verbatim
episcopal quotation until a primary source is read.

**Classification: MENTION WITHOUT GRANT** at most, and arguably not even
that — the single hit is a scriptural sentence, not a ceremonial
direction. **Verdict: VERIFIED** on incense; **UNMATCHED** on the 1929
declaration.

### 2(c) The Vestures of Ministers Measure 1964 and the Prayer Book (Alternative and Other Services) Measure 1965

**Both were read in full. Neither names incense, a censer, or a
thurible.** Captured at
`SRC_PRIMARY_1964-1974_ChurchAssembly-GeneralSynod_Measures_Vestures-AlternativeServices-WorshipAndDoctrine.txt`.

The 1964 Measure is about vesture and nothing else. It qualifies the
Ornaments Rubric and s.13 of the Act of Uniformity 1558 "subject to the
following" four lettered paragraphs — cassock, surplice, scarf, stole,
alb, cope, hood, "the customary vestments." Its preamble is worth
noting for a different argument: "the Church of England does not attach
any particular doctrinal significance to the diversities of vesture
which may lawfully be worn."

The 1965 Measure created the discretion that Canon B 5 now carries. Its
ss.5, 6, 7(1) and 7(2) are the near-verbatim ancestors of B 5 §§1, 2, 3
and 4. It never names incense.

Both were repealed **in whole** by the 1974 Measure, Schedule 2.

**Classification: not applicable to 1964** (no mention at all);
**PERMISSION BY SILENCE for 1965**. **Verdict: VERIFIED.**

### 2(d) The Worship and Doctrine Measure 1974

Read in full — seven sections and three Schedules. **It does not name
incense.** What it does:

- **s.1(1)** vests in the General Synod power to provide by Canon "with
  respect to worship in the Church of England," and for any matter to
  which a BCP rubric relates.
- **s.5(2)** defines "form of service" as "any order, service, prayer,
  rite or **ceremony whatsoever**."
- **Schedule 2** repeals the Act of Uniformity 1548 and the Act of
  Uniformity 1558 "so far as unrepealed," and the Act of Uniformity 1662
  **"except sections 10 and 15."**

⛔ **A repo claim narrowed.** Two live repo documents
(`RJ_Incense_Analysis.md`, `Incense_Conversational_Outline.md`) say the
1974 Measure "repealed the Act of Uniformity 1662." Schedule 2 says the
whole Act **except ss.10 and 15**. The claim is substantially right and
imprecisely stated. Anyone deploying it outward should carry the
exception, because an opponent who checks will find it. Recorded for a
future reconcile pass; not edited here.

**Classification: PERMISSION BY SILENCE.** The Measure removes the
statutory frame in which the 1899 Opinion reasoned and moves the power
of provision to Synod by Canon, without ever mentioning the practice.
**Verdict: VERIFIED.**

### 2(e) Canon B 5 — exact wording, and whether the discretion is bounded

Full text captured at
`SRC_PRIMARY_2026_ChurchOfEngland_Canon-B5-and-Section-F_Discretion-and-Ornaments.txt`.
**It does not name incense.** Sections B and F of the Canons were
retrieved in full and searched: **zero occurrences** of incense, censer
or thurible in either.

**The discretion is bounded, on its face, four ways:**

1. **By degree** — §1 reaches only variations "which are not of
   substantial importance."
2. **By occasion** — §2 reaches only "occasions for which no provision
   is made." It is a gap-filling power, not a general one.
3. **By content** — §3, "reverent and seemly," and "neither contrary to,
   nor indicative of any departure from, the doctrine of the Church of
   England in any essential matter."
4. **By reviewability** — §4, referable to the bishop for "pastoral
   guidance, advice or directions," expressly "without prejudice to the
   matter in question being made the subject matter of proceedings under
   the Ecclesiastical Jurisdiction Measure 1963."

Canon B 1 §2 frames it: "Every minister shall use only the forms of
service authorized by this Canon, except so far as he may exercise the
discretion permitted by Canon B 5."

**Canon F 1 - F 18, "Things appertaining to churches"**, is the Church
of England's own closed list of what a church must provide — font, holy
table, communion plate, linen, surplices, desks and pulpit, seats,
bells, Bible and BCP, alms box, registers. Incense apparatus is nowhere
in it. That is a negative over a complete list, not an open-ended
search, which is what gives it weight. The one condition F imposes that
could touch a censer is F 13 §3, requiring "the faculty or licence of
the Ordinary" for alterations or additions to "the fabric, ornaments, or
furniture of the church" — a condition on introducing the **object**,
not on the ceremonial act. Do not overstate it as episcopal consent to
censing.

**Classification: PERMISSION BY SILENCE.** **Verdict: VERIFIED.**

### 2(f) Do any Church of England service books name incense?

- **1928 proposed book** — no (2(b)).
- **ASB 1980** — incense is not named. The complete General Notes and
  all 24 Notes to Rite A were read; the nearest ceremonial note is Note
  16, "Manual Acts." ⛔ Rite B, Initiation, Marriage, Funerals and the
  Ordinal were not machine-readable anywhere found: **UNMATCHED**, not
  negative.
- **Common Worship** — one permissive rubric names incense, in *Daily
  Prayer*, The Blessing of the Light: **"Verses from Psalm 141 - A Song
  of the Evening Sacrifice may be said and incense may be burned."** No
  condition on its face: no bishop's direction, no limit of manner.

**This is the strongest candidate anywhere in the Church of England, and
the one an opponent will reach for. It does not hold as a grant, and the
reason is documentary.** The Church of England's own schedule appended
to the Canons distinguishes AUTHORIZED services from COMMENDED ones. Out
of the whole of *Common Worship: Daily Prayer*, the only item in the
Authorized list is **"26. The Calendar."** The **services** of *Daily
Prayer* appear instead under "Commended services and resources —
*Material commended by the House of Bishops as being suitable for use by
ministers in exercise of their discretion under Canon B 5*."

So the permission runs through Canon B 5, which never names incense. The
rubric describes what may happen inside a service used under a
discretion conferred elsewhere. It is not a Synod grant.

⛔ **Keep the contrary reading live.** A House of Bishops commendation is
an act of a body of the church, and material it commends which says
"incense may be burned" is at least an official statement that burning
incense is something a minister may lawfully do. That does not make it a
grant, but it is not frivolous, and JD should expect it to be pressed.

Weaker mentions, all **MENTION WITHOUT GRANT**: *Times and Seasons*,
"Nails or incense studs may be inserted into the Easter Candle" (an
incense *object*, not censing); Epiphany, incense as a *gift* at the
crib; the *Daily Prayer* General Introduction naming "the burning of
incense in a censer or a simple pot" as "obvious and natural"
(directory, not rubric); *New Patterns for Worship*, "The burning of
incense or aromatic oils to make the congregation smell 'sweeter'"
(commentary — and note it echoes the 1899 Opinion's own fumigatory
register, George Herbert's "to sweeten the church").

⛔ Roughly a dozen Common Worship nodes, **including Holy Communion Order
One itself**, returned empty on every retrieval variant. Those volumes
are index-negative only: **UNMATCHED**, not searched-and-clear.

**Classification: MENTION WITHOUT GRANT** for every occurrence.
**Verdict: PARTIAL** — verified for what was retrieved; genuinely
incomplete for ASB Rite B and several Common Worship volumes.

### §2 VERDICT

**VERDICT §2: VERIFIED — no positive authorization of incense by name
was found anywhere in the Church of England after 1900.** JD's claim
extends past 1900 for England.

**What does the permitting is Canon B 5**, made under s.1(5)(b) of the
Worship and Doctrine Measure 1974, exercised over ground cleared by
Schedule 2's repeal of the Acts of Uniformity — a general discretion as
to forms of service, bounded four ways, which has never named the
practice. **PERMISSION BY SILENCE**, exactly as the working belief
predicted.

⛔ **Do not report the current English position as settled.** Two
readings are live in the literature and both must be carried: that the
repeal of the 1662 frame ended the Victorian ceremonial case law, so
ceremonies not provided for are now prima facie permitted; and Bursell's
contrary reading, that ceremonies remain regulated by Canons B 1 - B 5,
on which a ceremony outside the authorized forms is no more lawful now
than it was. Philip Jones, who takes the first view, concedes Bursell
"may be" right. This is contested ground and should be reported as
contested.

## §3 — American Episcopal Church

### 3(a) Canon 20, Title I as enacted in 1874 — and its subsequent history

**Read from the existing repo capture first, as instructed**
(`src/SRC_PRIMARY_1874_GeneralConvention_Journal_Canon-20-Title-I_Ritual-Canon-Sequence.txt`).
The decisive fact is already in the repo and is worth restating because
it is so easily misremembered: **incense was struck out of the canon
before enactment.** It appears in the Committee's report of 23 October
as example (a), "The use of Incense"; in Blanchard's substitute; and in
Bolton's substitute. The House of Bishops then adopted the Bishop of
North Carolina's amendment "striking out the words beginning with 'And
as examples,' and ending with 'Book of Common Prayer'" — all four
examples. The Committee of Conference text that finally passed on 31
October 1874 illustrates only elevation of the elements, acts of
adoration toward the elements, and "All other like acts not authorized
or allowed by the Rubrics of the Book of Common Prayer."

**The enacted 1874 canon does not name incense.** Incense would have to
come in, if at all, under "all other like acts," bounded by "like."

**Its subsequent history: repealed in 1904.** Journal of the General
Convention 1904, p. 134, House of Bishops' journal, recording House of
Deputies Message No. 59 of 21 October 1904:

> "Resolved, the House of Bishops concurring, That Title I., Canon 24 of
> the Digest be and is hereby repealed."

Identified by White & Dykman, vol. 1 (1954), p. 411: "Title I, Canon 24,
Of the Use of the Book of Common Prayer with the ritual section, so
called, forbidding certain ceremonies and practices in the
administration of the Holy Communion … was repealed by this Convention,
and without a single voice being raised in its defense." A full-text
search of the 1904 Journal for "ritual" and for "ceremonies or
practices" returns zero: the canon was struck by number, silently.
**Nothing was enacted in its place.**

⚠ **Numbering trail — flagged, not resolved.** The repo's 1874 capture
header states that the Digest printed with the 1874 Journal renumbers
Canon 20 of Title I as **Canon 22**. This pass found the canon standing
as **Canon 21** in the c.1887 Digest and as **Canon 24** by 1904. These
are not necessarily in conflict — a Digest can renumber more than once —
but the chain 20 → 22 → 21 → 24 has not been verified end to end, and no
outward-facing document should assert any single number without
checking the Digest for the year it is talking about.

**Classification: the enacted canon is not a mention of incense at all;
its repeal is PERMISSION BY SILENCE.** **Verdict: VERIFIED.**

### 3(b) Has any American canon named incense since?

**No.** A full-text search of the current Constitution & Canons returned

**Classification: PERMISSION BY SILENCE.** **Verdict: PARTIAL** — the
finding is right on the sources read, but the canonical search was
truncated and should be redone against a complete text before deployment.

### 3(c) The 1892 and 1928 Prayer Books

**1892: incense appears once, as Scripture only** — Malachi 1:11 among
the Opening Sentences of Daily Morning Prayer, under the rubric "The
Minister shall always begin the Morning Prayer by reading one or more of
the following Sentences of Scripture." No 1892 rubric names incense.

**1928: incense appears five times, all Scripture; "ornament" appears

Prayer Sentences, p. 21); Psalms 66:13 and 141:2 in the Psalter; and
"frankincense," Matthew 2:11, in the Epiphany Gospel. *Censer*,
*thurible*, *vestment*, *chasuble*: zero. *Ornament*: zero in both
extraction modes and after de-hyphenation.

**This is the operative liturgical question for St Francis**, because §5
establishes that the EMC's Constitution makes the 1928 book its
standard. It was therefore checked directly against the text of that
book, not inferred.

⛔ **One method caveat that must travel with the 1928 zero.** The same

1928 zero is therefore strong but **not proof-grade** until a second

**Classification: MENTION WITHOUT GRANT** for both books — Scripture
only, no ceremonial rubric. **Verdict: PARTIAL**, on the strength of the
control failure.

### 3(d) The 1979 Book of Common Prayer

**Incense appears in two rubrics, both conditional, both unconditioned
as to permission.**

> p. 143, Additional Directions, An Order of Worship for the Evening:
> "If incense is to be used, it is appropriate after the candles have
> been lighted and while the hymn *Phos hilaron* is being sung."

> p. 576, Additional Directions, The Dedication and Consecration of a
> Church: "If incense is to be used, it is appropriate at this time."

Negative check: the Holy Eucharist Additional Directions (pp. 406-409)
contain no occurrence. Psalter occurrences are text to be said, not
rubrics.

**Classification: MENTION WITHOUT GRANT.** The form "*If* incense is to
be used" assumes the practice from outside the Book and regulates only
its placement. **No conditions attached** — no bishop's consent, no
restriction on manner or occasion. That absence of condition is itself a
§4 finding.

**Verdict: VERIFIED.**

### 3(e) The Book of Occasional Services

**This is where the claim comes closest to breaking, and it must be
reported precisely.** The BOS contains a complete liturgical form for
the dedication of a vessel for incense. From *The Book of Occasional
Services 2003*, p. 209, form 18 of "Dedication of Church Furnishings and
Ornaments":

> **18. A Vessel for Incense**
> *Antiphon* — Another angel came and stood at the altar with a golden
> censer; he was given much incense, and the smoke of the incense rose
> with the prayers of the saints.
> V. The four living creatures and the four and twenty elders fell down
> before the Lamb.
> R. Each held golden vessels full of incense, which are the prayers of
> the saints.
> *Let us pray.* (Silence)
> Almighty God, whose only-begotten Son received from the wise men a
> gift of incense and made for us the pure oblation foretold by the
> prophet: We dedicate to your worship this vessel, that our prayers may
> ascend in your sight as the incense, and the pure oblation of our Lord
> be proclaimed from farthest east to farthest west; through Jesus
> Christ our Lord. Amen.

**Conditions (§4):** form 18 carries **none**. Within the same section,
forms 1 (Altar) and 2 (Font) are "Reserved to the Bishop" and forms 3
and 4 "Traditionally reserved" — form 18 is neither. The section's
rubrics direct that the forms be used "after the sermon (and Creed) at
the Eucharist, or at the Daily Office at the time of the hymn or anthem
following the Collects," and that a portable object "should be brought
to the Altar and presented to the celebrant." That is placement, not
permission.

**Authorizing basis:** the BOS Preface states the material is
"authorized by the General Convention [through the provisions of Title
II, Canon 3, Section 6]," and that "All of the material in this book is
optional." Res. 1979-A055 authorized it "for optional use throughout
this Church," with no bishop's-permission condition on its face —
contrast Enriching Our Worship 6 and expansive-language Prayer C, which
do carry one.

**Classification — and the honest difficulty.** Strictly, this is a
**MENTION WITHOUT GRANT**: General Convention authorized a *form for
dedicating a vessel*, not the *use of incense in worship*. The form
blesses an object. It does not say a priest may cense.

But it is the strongest mention in either church by a wide margin, and
an opponent's reading is not unreasonable: a General-Convention-
authorized liturgy that prays "that our prayers may ascend in your sight
as the incense" over a vessel dedicated "to your worship" presupposes,
in an authorized text, that the vessel will be used in worship. **Both
readings are kept live here deliberately**, per the project's standing
rule. What can be said without qualification is the narrow thing: **no
American instrument grants the use of incense by name.** What cannot be
said without qualification is "the American church has never authorized
anything naming incense" — it has authorized a form whose entire subject
is a vessel for it.

⛔ **UNMATCHED: the 2018 wording.** The quoted form is from the **2003**
edition. The 2018 PDF truncated at ~p. 100; its table of contents does
confirm "Dedication of Church Furnishings and Ornaments 194," but the
2018 wording was not read. **Do not assume 2003 = 2018.** A trap is
logged in the capture file: a search-engine gloss claims the versicle is
"Let my prayer be set forth, O Lord / As incense in your sight." That
versicle exists elsewhere in the BOS; it is **not** form 18's.

**Verdict: PARTIAL** — the form is verified verbatim for 2003;
the 2018 text is unread; and the classification, while defensible as
MENTION WITHOUT GRANT, is the one finding in this pass where a competent
opponent has a real argument.

### §3 VERDICT

**VERDICT §3: PARTIAL — with the working belief falsified in one
respect.**

No American instrument positively authorizes the *use* of incense by
name. The 1874 ritual canon, which had named it in draft, was stripped
of the name before enactment and repealed entire in 1904 with nothing
put in its place; no canon has named incense since; the permitting is
done by the rector's control of worship, bounded by the Rubrics, the
Canons and the bishop's godly counsel. **PERMISSION BY SILENCE.**

**But the orchestration thread's working belief — that neither church
has ever positively authorized incense by name — is too strong as
stated for America.** The 1979 Book of Common Prayer names incense in
two rubrics, and the Book of Occasional Services contains a complete
General-Convention-authorized liturgical form whose subject is a vessel
for incense, dedicated by name, without condition. These are mentions,
not grants, and the claim survives if it is stated as *"no instrument
grants the use of incense."* **It does not survive if stated as "no
American instrument has ever named incense."** That distinction is now
load-bearing and should be built into how the claim is made.

## §4 — Conditions and limits

Recorded for every finding above that approaches authorization or
permission.

| Instrument | Condition attached |
|---|---|
| CoE, Canon B 5 (the actual permitting instrument in England) | **Bounded four ways.** Variations must be "not of substantial importance" (§1); the gap-filling power reaches only "occasions for which no provision is made" (§2); all use must be "reverent and seemly" and "neither contrary to, nor indicative of any departure from, the doctrine of the Church of England in any essential matter" (§3); and any question "may be referred to the bishop … for pastoral guidance, advice or directions," without prejudice to proceedings under the Ecclesiastical Jurisdiction Measure 1963 (§4). |
| CoE, Canon F 13 §3 | The **faculty or licence of the Ordinary** for additions to "the fabric, ornaments, or furniture" — a condition on introducing a censer as a church ornament, **not** on the ceremonial act. Do not overstate. |
| CoE, Common Worship: Daily Prayer, Blessing of the Light | **No condition in the rubric itself.** The condition is upstream and structural: the service is *commended*, not *authorized*, and is usable only under a minister's Canon B 5 discretion, with B 5's four bounds. |
| CoE, Worship and Doctrine Measure 1974 | Synod's power is bounded by s.1(1) — BCP forms must "continue to be available" — and by s.4(1)'s doctrine test. Neither touches incense. |
| TEC, BCP 1979 pp. 143 and 576 | **Unconditioned.** No ordinary's consent, no confinement beyond the service each rubric sits in, no limit stated on manner or occasion. The rubrics are conditional in form ("if incense is to be used") but impose no condition of permission. |
| TEC, BOS form 18, A Vessel for Incense | **Unconditioned.** Expressly *not* among the forms reserved to the bishop (forms 1-2) or traditionally reserved (3-4). Placement is directed; permission is not required. The BOS as a whole is "optional." |
| TEC, rector's control of worship (Title III.9.6) | Bounded three ways: "subject to the Rubrics of the Book of Common Prayer, the Canons of the Church, and the godly counsel of the Bishop." |
| EMC, Constitution Art. VII | Episcopal discretion bounded by "the Rubrics of the Book of Common Prayer or … the Canons of this Church"; alternative formularies require "the permission of the Bishop Ordinary of the Diocese" and must be "in conformity with the theology of the 1928 Book of Common Prayer." |
| EMC, rector | **No ceremonial discretion vested.** See §5(d). |

**The single most striking result in this section is a negative: where
the American texts name incense at all, they attach no condition to it.
Where the English regime permits it, it names nothing and attaches four
conditions to everything.**

**VERDICT §4: VERIFIED.**

### 5(a) Origin

The EMC's own About page states it was "incorporated in 1992, [and] is
an outgrowth of the Episcopal Synod of America (now Forward in Faith,
North America)."

⛔ **UNMATCHED: what it stated about its relation to the instruments of
the church it left.** No published EMC instrument addresses ECUSA's
canonical or liturgical instruments. The Constitution and Canons nowhere
name ECUSA. The only article touching relation is Article VIII, and it
is confined to property and temporal jurisdiction — each church, diocese
and parish holds title in its own name "and shall be immune to the
temporal claim or jurisdiction of any other body, corporation, or
person, civil or ecclesiastical." That is a trust-clause defense, not a
statement about canons or the Prayer Book. Searched: the full
constitution, all four canon titles, and the complete site map — there
is no preamble, declaration, or history page.

**Verdict: PARTIAL** — the fact of formation is sourced; the
instrument-relation statement is UNMATCHED and must not be inferred.

### 5(b) Which Prayer Book — and on what authority

**Constitutional, not merely practice.** Constitution Article VII: the
1928 Book of Common Prayer "shall be in use in all parishes of this
Church." Restated at Canon 13, a single sentence: "According to Article
VII of the Constitution, the 1928 Book of Common Prayer shall be the
standard Book of Common Prayer in this Church."

Note the EMC's own website hedges where its instruments do not —
"predominantly uses." **The instruments govern.**

**Verdict: VERIFIED.**

### 5(c) Canons and discipline; incense and ceremonial

Canons adopted 9 October 1998. Discipline is Title IV of the Canons
(canons 29-36); there is no separately-titled book of discipline. All 36
canons were enumerated from the site's own sitemap, so this is a
**negative over a known-complete index**, not an unbounded search.

**No canon in the set names incense, ceremonial, ritual, ornaments, or
vesture.** Title II, "Worship," is only three canons: Bible
translations, the Prayer Book, and music. Canon 29 (Of Offenses) reaches
doctrine — "Holding or teaching publicly or privately and advisedly, any
doctrine contrary to that held by this Church" — and neglect, but not
ceremonial as such.

⛔ **One live gap.** Canon 04 §3 requires each diocese to have its own
canons, and Canon 29 §1(4) makes violating them an offense. **No EMC
diocesan canon set is published anywhere reachable.** A diocesan
ceremonial provision is therefore **unknown, not disproved**. This is
the one place where a governing instrument bearing on St Francis could
exist and has not been read.

**Classification: PERMISSION BY SILENCE.** **Verdict: PARTIAL**, on the
diocesan gap.

### 5(d) Where ceremonial discretion is vested, and whether bounded

**It is not vested in the rector.** This is the sharpest finding in §5.

Article VII bounds **episcopal** discretion twice. First, nothing in the
article "shall be construed as restricting the authority of the Bishops
of this Church to take such order as may be permitted by the Rubrics of
the Book of Common Prayer or by the Canons of this Church for the
special forms of worship" — the bound is the Rubrics and the Canons.
Second, the alternative-liturgy clause vests discretion in the **bishop**
under a doctrinal test: "With the permission of the Bishop Ordinary of
the Diocese, other formularies in conformity with the theology of the
1928 Book of Common Prayer may be used."

The only "final authority" the canons give a rector over worship is
Canon 14, and it is expressly **music**: "he shall be the final
authority in the administration of matters pertaining to music,"
bounded by a duty — "It shall be his duty to suppress all unseemly music
and all irreverence in the rendition thereof." Canon 07 §3 gives the
rector "the use and control of the church and parish buildings with the
appurtenances and furniture thereof" — property, not ceremonial — and
§4 subjects him generally: "said Rector shall be subject to all the
provisions of these canons."

⭐ **Worth noting for argument design, and stated as this project's own
Analysis, not as an EMC position:** Canon 12 shows that the EMC knows
how to write a rector-needs-the-bishop's-permission clause, and did so
for Bible translations. It wrote none for ceremonial. The EMC's canonical
architecture on worship is markedly thinner than TEC's — it has no
equivalent of Title III.9.6's grant of "control of the worship" to the
rector.

**Verdict: VERIFIED.**

### 5(e) THE HINGE — does the 1874 canon form part of the EMC's baseline?

**No. It forms no part of it, in any form.** Three independent grounds:

1. EMC Title I ends at Canon 11. **"Canon 20, Title I" does not exist in
   the EMC set.**
2. EMC Canon 20 is "Of Ordination to the Priesthood," in Title III — a
   different canon entirely under the same number.
3. Canon 36 (Of Enactment, Amendment, and Repeal) contains **no
   reception or incorporation-by-reference clause** of any prior canon
   set. Its §2 is an *anti*-revival rule: a repealed canon "shall not
   thereby be revived or re-enacted, without express words to that
   effect."

**Verdict: VERIFIED (negative).**

**What this means for the occasion of the pass.** The canon RJ cites is
**not** carried into the EMC's canonical inheritance, so its history and
enacted wording bear on St Francis **by analogy only**, not directly.
The brief flagged this as the hinge, and the hinge turns the other way
from the way it was set up.

That said, the analogy is not weak — it is just an analogy, and it now
runs through a *different* route. The EMC's operative liturgical
instrument is the **1928 American Book of Common Prayer**, made standard
by Article VII of its own Constitution. And §3(c) establishes, by direct
search of the text of that book, that **incense appears in it five
times, every one of them Scripture, and in no rubric.** That is the
finding that bears directly on the parish. The 1874 canon does not.

### §5 VERDICT

**VERDICT §5: PARTIAL.**

Established: the EMC's Prayer Book is the 1928 book, constitutionally
(VERIFIED); no EMC canon names incense or ceremonial, over a
known-complete index (VERIFIED, subject to the diocesan gap); ceremonial
discretion is not vested in the rector, and what discretion exists is
episcopal and bounded (VERIFIED); the 1874 canon is not part of the EMC
baseline (VERIFIED, negative).

**UNMATCHED and not to be inferred:** the EMC's stated relation at
formation to the instruments of the church it left; and any EMC
**diocesan** canon set, which Canon 04 §3 requires to exist and which is
not published anywhere reachable. An honest UNMATCHED on both, per the
brief.

## Answer to the question the pass was set

**JD's claim extends past 1900, but only in its precise form.**

*"Liturgical incense has never been positively authorized by name"* —
**holds**, in both churches, to the present. No instrument in either
church grants the use of incense.

*"No instrument has ever named incense"* — **breaks**, and breaks in
America, not England. It breaks at the **1979 Book of Common Prayer**,
pp. 143 and 576, which name incense in two Additional Directions; and
more sharply at the **Book of Occasional Services**, form 18, "A Vessel
for Incense," an authorized liturgical form whose entire subject is
incense, dedicated by name, with no condition attached.

**The regime thesis holds for England and needs qualifying for
America.** In England the change is exactly as the working belief
described: litigated lawfulness gave way to a general, bounded
discretion that has never named the practice, over ground cleared by the
1974 repeal of the Acts of Uniformity. In America the change is
different in kind — the prohibition-shaped canon was repealed outright
in 1904 with nothing put in its place, and the authorized liturgical
books then began, from 1979, to *name* incense while still never
granting it.

**For St Francis specifically**, neither the 1874 canon nor the 1979
book nor the BOS is in force. The EMC's instruments make the **1928
American Book of Common Prayer** its standard, that book names incense
only in Scripture and in no rubric, no EMC canon touches ceremonial, and
ceremonial discretion — such as it is — sits with the bishop, not the
rector.
