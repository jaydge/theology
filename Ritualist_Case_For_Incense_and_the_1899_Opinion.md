## 1. What the Ornaments Rubric actually says

The rubric stands immediately before Morning Prayer in the 1662 Book of Common Prayer. Its second paragraph is the operative one:

> "And here is to be noted, that such Ornaments of the Church, and of the Ministers thereof, at all Times of their Ministration, shall be retained, and be in use, as were in this Church of England, by the Authority of Parliament, in the Second Year of the Reign of King Edward the Sixth."

Four facts about its status matter, and all four are agreed by both sides:

1. **It is statutory, not merely rubrical.** The 1662 Prayer Book is annexed to and incorporated by the Act of Uniformity 1662 (14 Car. II c. 4), so the rubric carries the force of a statute. The ritualist submission leans on this hard (see §3d).
2. **It is not new in 1662.** It first appears in the Elizabethan Prayer Book of 1559, was retained in 1604, and is essentially an extract from the penultimate section of the Act of Uniformity 1559 (1 Eliz. I c. 2) — **an extract that breaks off mid-sentence.**
3. **What it breaks off from is decisive.** The 1559 Act's full clause continues: such ornaments shall be retained and in use *"until other order shall be therein taken by the Queen's Majesty with the advice of her commissioners… or of the metropolitan of this realm."* **The 1662 rubric drops the proviso; the statute it derives from contains it.** Everything in the legal history turns on whether "other order" was ever taken.
4. **"The second year of the reign of King Edward the Sixth"** runs from 28 January 1548 to 27 January 1549 — Edward having succeeded on 28 January 1547. Whether that phrase points to the 1549 Prayer Book or to the state of things *before* it is the second great dispute (§2c).

**Source:** rubric and 1559 Act text, [Ornaments Rubric, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ornaments_Rubric); statutory-force point, *The Case for Incense* (1899), p. 24; regnal-year arithmetic corroborated by both the ritualist submission (quoting Micklethwaite) and the Archbishops' Opinion.

## 2. The ritualist case, on its own strongest terms

The best single statement of it is the 1899 submission itself, because it was written by the party's own experts to be read by a judge who was expected to be unsympathetic. Its named appendix authors were **W. H. Frere**, **W. J. Birkbeck**, **H. R. Percival**, **Darwell Stone**, **E. Geldart**, **T. A. Lacey** and **W. H. St John Hope** — that is, the front rank of Anglo-Catholic liturgical scholarship at the time.

### 2a. The two-filter reading of the rubric

The submission does **not** argue that "ornaments" means "ceremonies." That claim was available and they deliberately declined it. Their structure is subtler:

> "The second year of Edward VI. is fixed as the date which in the first place determines what ornaments are to be retained, and the ministrations imposed upon the clergy by the Prayer Book are fixed upon in the second place, as the limit for determining which of those ornaments are to be in use." (p. 9)

So: **filter one**, was the object lawful in the second year? **Filter two**, is the ministration it attaches to still prescribed? The censer passes both —

> "The Approach to the Altar, the Gospel, the Offertory, and the Magnificat are all ministrations prescribed by the Prayer Book. They are all ministrations at which a censer and Incense were accustomed to be used." (p. 12)

For "ornaments" they take the Privy Council's own definition from *Liddell v. Westerton* — "the same dresses and the same utensils, or articles, which were used under the First Prayer Book of Edward VI. may still be used" — which puts a censer inside the category as an object, without any need to stretch the word.

### 2b. How ceremonial re-enters — the hinge of the whole case

Having won the object, they carry the ceremony in with it:

> "But the accustomed use of a censer includes the censing of persons and things, and, in default of any prohibition and of any fresh directions, it must be assumed that the ornament, when (as the rubric directs) it is retained and in use, should be used in the old way." (p. 12)

And pre-empting the fatal objection — that this is an *additional ceremony*, which *Martin v. Mackonochie* held forbidden — they plead in the alternative:

> "…the use of them is not an additional ceremony, but is either not a ceremony at all but only a subsidiary detail of the act of worship or entire ceremony composing the Service in which it is used, or, if a ceremony, is one allowed, if not prescribed, by the Rubric and Statute as above construed…" (p. 24)

⭐ **This is the joint on which the case lives or dies, and it is worth naming precisely.** English ecclesiastical law of the period ran two different rules: **ceremonies** not retained by the Prayer Book were treated as abolished (the Preface *Of Ceremonies* — "why some be abolished, and some retained"), whereas the **Ornaments Rubric**, on its wording, *required* some ornaments without positively forbidding others. The whole ritualist strategy is to get the censer classified as an **ornament** and its swinging as an incident of the ornament's use, rather than as a **ceremony** in its own right.

### 2c. The date argument — and the refusal to depend on it

The submission quotes J. T. Micklethwaite, *The Ornaments of the Rubric* (Alcuin Club, 1897), at length. The argument: the 1549 Book received parliamentary authority on 21 January 1549 — inside the second year — but by the Act's own terms could not be *used* before Whitsunday, 9 June 1549, which is the **third** year. So the rubric cannot mean the 1549 Book; it must mean what was lawfully in use through the second year, which was the old Latin Mass as modified by the *Order of Communion* of 8 March 1548 — an Order that expressly left the Mass to proceed *"without varying of any other rite or ceremony."*

Then the move that makes the argument robust rather than brittle:

> "But whether the reference to the second year means the second year, or whether it refers to the First Prayer Book, is immaterial to the present argument. There is no doubt that Incense was used in the accustomed manner in the second year of Edward VI. There is no reasonable doubt that it was so used under the First Prayer Book. There is not a word in Edward VI.'s First Prayer Book to the effect that Incense shall not be used as heretofore." (p. 10)

⚠️ **Note what this does: it concedes the harder half of the date dispute in advance and still stands.** Incense was lawful on *either* reading. This is why the Archbishops, in 1899, were able to decide the case without resolving the date question at all (§5).

### 2d. Silence is permissive, not prohibitive

This is the spine of the submission, and it is argued from four directions.

**(i) The rubrics were never an exhaustive code.**

> "…the fact of such omission cannot be held to involve a prohibition, unless it can be proved that the rubrics of the various editions of the Book of Common Prayer either are, or were intended to be, in any sense an exhaustive and complete set of directions for the performance of Divine Service." (p. 6)

> "The rubrics in the Missal and Breviary made no pretension to such completeness… in the Hereford Missal no mention is made of Incense except at the Gospel, and in no English Missal are the lights on the Altar prescribed, any more than they are in the Prayer Book. But it would be very perilous to argue in consequence that Incense was not used at the Offertory according to the use of Hereford…" (p. 6)

**(ii) Where the Reformers meant to prohibit, they said so.** They produce three prohibitory rubrics as exemplars — Mattins "without any Invitatory"; the 1549 Communion's "without any elevation, or shewing the Sacrament to the people"; the bread to be "without all manner of print" — and argue that the absence of any comparable prohibition of incense is therefore meaningful. (pp. 10–11)

**(iii) The reductio.** This is the most quotable passage in the whole submission:

> "At the present time it is not doubted by anyone that the use of coloured altar cloths, and the cross, and credence table, and candlesticks, and turning to the East during the reading of the Creeds, and the saying of 'Glory be to Thee, O Lord' before the reading of the Gospel, and the expression of thanks after it, and the use of hymns, are legal, although each would be unlawful if it were true that omission is equivalent to prohibition." (p. 25)

> "…the First Prayer Book of Edward VI… contained various directions concerning the administration of the Holy Communion; but no reference to 'a fair linen cloth.' If only that which is expressly permitted, or directed, is lawful, the use of a 'fair linen cloth' at the time of the Celebration would have been illegal in the year 1549." (p. 25)

**(iv) The tu quoque aimed at the bench.** A footnote (note 35) turns the argument on the tribunal's own regalia:

> "If the Bishop's pastoral staff may be ceremonially used in the accustomed manner, let it be remembered that the use of Incense stands on exactly the same ground. The authority for the crosses borne before the Archbishops of Canterbury and York, and for Bishops' mitres, is equally good for censers; for it is to be noted that the latter have no authorization unless the ornaments rubric legalizes ornaments beyond those expressly mentioned in the First Prayer Book."

**Legal support marshalled:** Coke — "a Statute made in the affirmative without any negative, express or implied, doth not take away the common law" (2 Inst. 200); Blackstone on concurrent affirmative statutes; and 25 Hen. VIII c. 19 s. 7, which preserved pre-existing "Canons, Constitutions, Ordinances, and Synodals Provincial" not repugnant to the law of the realm, and is used to give incense *parliamentary* authority in Edward's second year indirectly.

**On desuetude:**

> "Now a Statute loses none of its validity by disuse… Long continued desuetude of a Statute may be a very cogent, and perhaps a conclusive, argument against enforcing obedience to its provisions. It cannot make that unlawful which the Statute either enjoins or permits." (p. 24)

### 2e. The appeal to catholic continuity

> "The Church of England, throughout all the changes of the Reformation period, steadily, and without flinching, has declared her intention and wish to retain all that was Catholic and primitive, and merely to get rid of what was superstitious, or had been abused to superstitious practice." (p. 4)

The burden-of-proof claim, which is the exact form of the continuity argument:

> "It is not claimed that no alteration of ceremonial took place at the Reformation; but that such alterations as were made were made on conservative lines, and only in view of certain specific and well-defined abuses; and, further, it is claimed that clear proof of such alteration is to be required before any innocent portion of the old ceremonial is held to be abrogated." (p. 4)

They then turn the Prayer Book's own Preface against the objectors: incense has not "been turned to vanity and superstition," is not "a burden which is intolerable," and is well calculated "to stir up the dull mind of man to the remembrance of his duty to God by… signification" — all three phrases lifted from the Preface itself. They cite the 1661 bishops' *Reply to the Exceptions* of the Presbyterian brethren, which condemns "a causeless departure from Catholick usage" and invokes "that golden rule of the venerable Council of Nice, Let ancient customs prevail, till reason plainly requires the contrary."

**And the internal-evidence argument from the 1549 Book's own vocabulary** — "the vesture appointed for that ministration," "the accustomed blessing," banns asked "after the accustomed manner," the churched woman's "accustomed offerings," Confirmation "agreeable with the usage of the Church in times past" — is deployed to show that the 1549 compilers were assuming a continuing body of custom, not writing on a blank sheet.

### 2f. The evidentiary case — what survived, and where

⚠️ **The detailed evidence sat in appendices not retrieved.** What survives in the portion read:

- **The 1552–3 confiscation reframed.** Censers were called in with *all* church plate "with the simple object of replenishing the King's exchequer, and not as an ecclesiastical matter" — supported by the Privy Council decree of March 1550[-1] (Dasent, *Acts of the Privy Council*, N.S. iii, p. 225) reciting that "the Kinge's Majestie had neede presently of a masse of mooney."
- **The inventory statistic.** Of goods in 1,402 churches across twelve counties, censers are recorded as retained in **378 (27%)** and sold in only **41 (barely 3%)**. (Attributed to St John Hope's Appendix G, not retrieved.)
- **Named Elizabethan inventories:** Lincolnshire 1566 (Peacock, *English Church Furniture*, pp. 53, 92); **Wymondham, 1 December 1564** — "Itm One shippe of tyne and censer of latten"; **Poole, Dorset, 28 November 1559** — "One crosse, one peare of sensers and one shippe of brasse," in a parish certified as conforming to "the boke of comon prayer and the quene's maties injuncions"; **Chelmsford 1560**; **St Edmunds, Sarum, 1567–8**.
- **The Bodmin indenture, 1566**, argued as proof of intended continuing *use* rather than mere custody: goods including "a ship of tin, and a censer of latten" handed to the churchwardens "to be used and occupied to the honour of God in the same church."
- **A post-1559 usage catena:** the Epiphany offering at the Chapel Royal (which they themselves call "by no means the strongest piece of evidence"); **Lancelot Andrewes'** chapel — "a triquertral censer wherein the clerk putteth frankincense at the reading of the First Lesson"; a church consecrated at **Wolverhampton in 1635** — "and so they went into the chancel where was Incense burned"; **Bishop Cosin**, who used incense and "approved of ceremonial censing"; **Archbishop Sancroft's 1685 consecration form**, which has a censer presented with "Let my prayer be set forth in Thy sight as the Incense"; **Ely Cathedral**, where bills for incense twice yearly survive among the Dean and Chapter's papers and the practice is said to have ceased c. 1778–80; and the coronation of George III, with the King's Groom of the Vestry "holding a perfuming pan."

⭐⭐ **How they handle the liturgical/fumigatory distinction is the most careful thing in the document, and it is NOT a denial.** They concede it:

> "…the fact that it was so used, and not always in the traditional way, merely shows that the traditional use had, in some places, been forgotten; it does not show that it had ever been forbidden, and still less does it authorize the irregular uses of incense which were adopted in ignorance of the true traditional use." (p. 13)

⛔ **The non-ceremonial instances are offered as NEGATIVE evidence only — that nobody thought incense prohibited — and the pleaders expressly decline to rest their own claim on them.** Any refutation that treats them as claiming George Herbert for the ceremonial use is attacking a position they did not take.

### 2g. Scripture, and an honest concession about the Fathers

The scriptural case is the one most likely to be recognisable: incense divinely enjoined in the Temple (Ex. 30:7–8; Lev. 16:12), Zechariah at the hour of incense chosen as the opening of the new dispensation, the Magi's gift, and Revelation 5:8 and 8:3–4 as heavenly worship — "it would be difficult to name any adjunct of worship for which more direct authority can be found in the Bible" (p. 22). On the Magi's gift they add a distinctive point: myrrh symbolised the Passion and may be held obsolete, but incense symbolises Christ's priestly intercession, and so can no more be dropped than the gold retained in the Offertory.

⭐⭐⭐ **But Frere's Appendix A concedes the patristic case outright, and this is the single most important thing in the document for anyone assessing it:**

> "The only conclusion to be drawn is then that the liturgical use of Incense is not attested during the first three centuries, except in so far as its use in funerals can be called liturgical. The result is remarkable, because it might well have been expected a priori that the Christian Church would have followed the precedent set alike by the Old and by the New Testament." (p. 31)

He surveys Athenagoras, Tertullian, Clement, Origen, Arnobius, Lactantius and Augustine **as evidence against** liturgical use, and explains the gap by the association of incense with the imperial cult — "the burning of incense at a tripod before a statue or a picture of the Emperor was the overt act of apostasy." He also volunteers the negative evidence: no mention in the Liturgy of the *Apostolic Constitutions*, none in Cyril of Jerusalem's *Catechetical Lectures*.

⚠️ **This concession is strategically intelligent, not naive.** It immunises the case against the charge of manufacturing patristic proof and shifts the entire load onto the legal argument — which needs only that incense was lawful in Edward's second year and never since prohibited, and needs no patristic antiquity at all. **Anyone who "refutes" the ritualist case by showing the primitive Church did not use incense has refuted a claim the ritualists' own expert made first, in their own submission.**

> ⭐⭐⭐ **DATED ADDITION, 260835-34 — THE CONCESSION IN ITS FULL CONTEXT, AND WHAT FRERE CLAIMS INSTEAD. The paragraph above is confirmed, located precisely, and shown to be considerably stronger than a single quotation suggests.**
>
> ⚠️ **First, two locating corrections.** (a) The concession is in **Appendix A** — *Notes on the Early History of the Use of Incense* — as §2g already says, and it stands on printed **p. 47**, not p. 31 (see the §0 dated note on pagination). (b) ⛔ **It is NOT in Appendix H.** Appendix H is Frere's *other* contribution, on post-Reformation uses (pp. 158–170, §2f-bis). Frere wrote three of the nine appendices — A, E and H — and the patristic concession belongs to the first.
>
> **What he concedes, and in what terms.** The concession is not an aside; **it is the conclusion of a sustained case that Frere builds against his own side**, running from p. 44 to p. 48. He marshals **Athenagoras** (*Legatio* 13), **Tertullian** three times (*Apologeticus* xxx and xlii, *De Corona* x), **Clement of Alexandria** twice (*Stromateis* vii. 6, *Paedagogus* ii. 8), **Origen** twice (*Contra Celsum* vii. 17, viii. 20), **Arnobius**, **Lactantius** and **St Augustine** — and reads all of them as evidence *against* liturgical use, not merely as silence:
>
> > "These passages show clearly that the Christian use of Incense in the second century was confined (so far as the evidence goes) to funerals, where it was used primarily for sanitary and not for liturgical purposes."
>
> > "…in the absence of any such evidence during the earlier period such passages as these can only be taken as **disproving (so far as they go) the liturgical use of Incense.**"
>
> He then adds the negative argument from the patristic handling of Malachi i. 11 — *"the allusion to Incense is either passed over in silence or explained as referring to prayer"* — before the conclusion quoted above. ⭐ **And he immediately declines the obvious escape route that was available to him**, stating the *argumentum e silentio* objection and then refusing to lean on it:
>
> > "In view of the scantiness of the evidence available about any of the liturgical customs of the first three centuries, it is open to anyone to hold that this lack of attestation is not in itself a strong argument. But, on the other hand, there is a fact ready to hand to serve as a presumptive explanation why the Christian Church should have thought best to forego the liturgical use of Incense…"
>
> **The explanation he offers has two limbs, not one.** The familiar one is the imperial cult — *"the burning of incense at a tripod before a statue or a picture of the Emperor was the overt act of apostasy… to consent was to deny Christ, to refuse was equivalent to courting martyrdom."* ⭐ **The second is rarely quoted and is the more damaging of the two to any patristic argument for incense:**
>
> > "There are signs, also, that in the early days the Church was not greatly disposed to follow Jewish lines, and therefore **the Biblical authority for incense, so far as it was drawn from Levitical ceremonial, would have told against the use of incense rather than for it.**"
>
> ⛔⛔ **THREE FURTHER CONCESSIONS IN THE SAME APPENDIX, NONE OF THEM PREVIOUSLY RECORDED IN THIS FILE, AND EACH GIVES AWAY MORE GROUND.**
>
> 1. **The liturgical use is late even after the Church accepts incense.** Of the post-Constantinian period: *"At first, however, there is very little trace to be found of its use ceremonially."* And flatly: *"Documentary evidence from liturgical books only seriously begins at the close of the seventh, or the opening of the eighth century."*
> 2. **Altar-censing specifically is a late development.** *"We shall, later on, see reason to believe that the full ceremony of censing the Altar was one of the later forms."*
> 3. ⭐⭐ **He destroys his own best patristic text.** He introduces St Ambrose on Luke i. 11 as *"The most crucial passage"* — and then rules against it: *"This is, however, very far from proving that, in using the term* adolentibus altaria*, he had in mind any censing of the Altar, or, indeed, any use of Incense, for, apart from this context and the use of the term in the Vulgate, the phrase does not naturally refer to Incense at all, but is a general sacrificial term."* He opens the next section *"holding our judgment in suspense with regard to the meaning of the passage in St. Ambrose."* He treats an Origen metaphor the same way: *"it is unsafe to build upon the passage, because we have no other witness to the use of Incense in the IIIrd century."*
>
> ⭐⭐⭐ **AND HERE IS WHAT HE CLAIMS INSTEAD. It is the last paragraph of the appendix, it is the answer to the whole question, and it converts what §2g inferred into something Frere states outright:**
>
> > "The history of the use of Incense at the Eucharist and the Divine Office presents **a good many difficulties, and still more gaps**; but the result finally arrived at by the time to which reference is made in the Prayer Book is in both cases clear… But it is only necessary to point out here that, in both cases, **the use of Incense which was customary in the second year of Edward VI. is beyond all doubt or question, and that, whatever history lies behind, it is this only that is of practical importance and legal value.**"
>
> ⭐ **"Whatever history lies behind, it is this only that is of practical importance and legal value."** That is the ritualists' own senior liturgist telling the Archbishop, in the submission, that **the patristic question is legally irrelevant** and that the case rests entirely on the state of use in 1548–9 — which he then documents from Lanfranc's *Decreta* and the Sarum Consuetudinary, closing: *"These are the regulations that were in force in the Second Year of Edward VI."*
>
> ⚠️⚠️ **ONE CONSEQUENCE THAT RUNS THE OTHER WAY, AND IT IS THE MOST INTERESTING THING IN THE APPENDIX.** Frere's developmental thesis is that the liturgical use **grew out of** the funerary and prophylactic use — *"the first step to be taken was to graft upon this utilitarian custom a symbolical and spiritual meaning"*; of processional, funerary and sick-visiting use together, *"all these three probably were in their origin sanitary precautions."* ⛔ **The 1899 Opinion's decisive evidentiary move is the clean binary between liturgical and fumigatory use — strike out the fumigatory instances and "the number of cases in which incense was used in worship is utterly insufficient." Frere's history says the two categories are genetically continuous, not disjoint.** ⭐ **That does not rescue the Elizabethan churchwardens' entries — Frere himself classes those as perfuming (§2f-bis) — but it does mean the Archbishops' sorting rule is one the ritualists' own historian had already shown to be historically naive. Recorded as an available line of argument, NOT as an established refutation: nobody in 1899 made it, and it appears nowhere in the submission.**

### 2h. ⭐⭐ The remaining appendices — what each contributes (dated addition, 260835-34)

> ⛔ **Recorded because the brief required every appendix to be accounted for, and because the pattern across all nine is itself the finding: the ritualists' experts concede against their own interest in every single appendix.** Page numbers are the book's printed folios (see the §0 dated note).

**Appendix B — W. J. Birkbeck, *On the Use of Incense in the Orthodox Eastern Church* (pp. 87–88).** The shortest appendix, and purely defensive: it **de-Romanises incense.** Birkbeck maps the Eastern uses point-for-point onto the four the English petitioners claim — *"immediately before the commencement of the Liturgy, before the reading of the Gospel, at the placing of the oblations upon the Altar, at Magnificat and Benedictus, and in Processions"* — so that the practice cannot be characterised as a Roman importation. ⚠️ **Concedes: "The circumstances, however, differ in detail"; that Eastern censing at the approach is done by the deacon, not the priest; that it "may never be administered by anyone who is not at least in deacon's orders"; and that "There is no censing at the Consecration."** He retreats from identity of practice to identity of principle: *"however much the actual practice may differ in detail, it may fairly be said that the principles underlying its use are the same in East and West."*

**Appendix C — H. R. Percival and Darwell Stone, *The Theological Value of Incense* (pp. 89–101).** Two treatises. **Percival's** governing move is to deny that ceremonies carry inherent meaning — *"no ceremonial act has any inherent meaning, its meaning being entirely dependent upon the intention with which it is performed… It is a complete mistake to suppose that the burning of Incense necessarily is an act of Divine worship, or has any necessary connection with sacrifice"* — which dissolves the sacrificial objection at a stroke, and he construes censing as testimony *against* repeated propitiation. ⚠️ **He concedes the heathen derivation outright** (*"This heathen-derived idea of Incense seems to have been sanctified by the early Church"*), disclaims Mosaic authority (*"its use does not rest upon the authority of the Mosaic law, but upon that of the Christian Church alone"*), and — most dangerously — concedes the abstract ground on which it *could* lawfully be abolished: *"unless, indeed, it had become so associated with false doctrine as to be perilous to the purity of the faith of the people."* **Stone** argues from the censing of persons as a specifically Christian development, and closes with a prudential fourth thesis about union with the East. ⛔⛔ **Stone supplies the sharpest concession in the appendix, and it duplicates Frere's independently: *"In the earliest Christian worship Incense was apparently not used"*, and *"When Incense came into use it is impossible to say in what manner it was used at the first."*** ⭐ **So the patristic concession of §2g is made TWICE, by two different experts, in two different appendices.**

**Appendix D — Ernest Geldart, *The Rubrics Compared and their Evidential Value Considered* (pp. 103–135).** The longest appendix and the **evidential substructure of §2d(i)**. Two parts: a four-column synoptic table of the rubrics of 1548, 1549, 1552 and 1559 (pp. 104–122, printed sideways and largely unreadable in this scan), into which Geldart inserts bracketed `[Incense.]` markers at the Introit, Gospel, Offertory and Canon; then an essay stating a testable rule of rubrical construction — *"Rubrics were inserted in the Service only where they were deemed necessary,"* with length proportional to the magnitude and contentiousness of the change. Therefore 1549's silence about incense means the accustomed use continued. ⭐ **His strongest argument is the *a fortiori* from triviality: "That the men who ruled that the wafer should be a trifle 'thicker and larger' in circumference should so decree on the 'infinitely little,' and at the same time pass over the burning of incense and the use of lights and crucifix in absolute silence, if they intended to abolish them, passes human understanding."** ⚠️⚠️ **Concedes, and these are serious:** a footnote to his own `[Incense]` marker at the Canon admits *"This was not, however, a general use, nor is it ordered by old rubrics"* (recovered from rotated OCR — attachment to the Canon marker is very likely but not certain); that silence sometimes *is* prohibitive (*"The silence of the Rubric in the matter of the Gradual is clearly proscriptive"*); that one rubric is simply careless (*"This is without question an oversight"*), which dents his own premise that nothing is capricious; and, on 1552, ⛔ *"The silence as to lights, incense, and gestures (crossing, &c.), does not imply approval, but rather **the fact of all such things (or nearly all) having ceased between 1550 and 1552.**"* **That is the ritualists' own rubrical expert dating the practical extinction of incense to within three years of the second year of Edward VI.**

**Appendix E — W. H. Frere, *Two Cases Illustrative of the Permanence of Accustomed Usages* (pp. 137–140).** An argument by analogy from two usages that survived without any Prayer Book warrant: **(i) change of Christian name at Confirmation**, from Peckham's constitution of 1281 through Coke (Sir Francis Gawdy, upheld "by the advice of all the Judges in Anno 36 H. 8") down to an 1886 Liverpool case; **(ii) the white veil at churching**, in *Eliz. Shipden v. Dr. Redman* (James I). ⭐ **The second is the real prize, because it is a legal precedent and not merely a survival: a woman excommunicated for refusing the veil sought a prohibition on the express ground that the order "was not warranted by any Custom or Canon of the Church of England"; the judges consulted the Archbishop, the bishops certified it "the ancient usage of the Church of England," and "a prohibition was denied."** That is precisely the posture the petitioners want from Temple. ⚠️ **Concedes that the veil "is not now generally worn," so his own case is one of *revival* — which is what opponents call innovation — and prints Dr Burn's contrary authority without refuting it.** ⛔ **Structural weakness, recorded because Frere does not address it: neither case concerns an *ornament*, and neither is a ceremony added to a prescribed service. One is a name and one is a garment.**

**Appendix F — T. A. Lacey, *The Use of the Censer after the Accustomed Manner* (pp. 141–149).** ⭐⭐ **This is the appendix that carries §2b, and it does the load-bearing work of the whole submission.** Lacey states the killing objection himself, at full strength — *"There is no provision for its use in the rites of the Book of Common Prayer; there is no ministration to which it can serve as an adjunct… The use of it, therefore, involves an additional ceremony"* — and answers it with a definitional distinction between a **ceremony** and a **ceremonial adjunct**: *"I contend, in reply, that the use of the censer after the ancient manner is not an additional ceremony, but is an adjunct to a ceremony ordered by the Book of Common Prayer."* The definition that carries it: a ceremony is *"something which is to be done by the Minister as an integral part of the prescript order. The censing was not a ceremony of this kind. It was never regarded as an integral part of any service. The service could be performed, and in practice would seem to have been most frequently performed, without it."* His best independent evidence is a c. 1537 Cotton MS exposition of the ceremonies of the Mass which explains every part of the rite and yet *"in the whole document there is no mention of Incense"* — offered as proof that contemporaries did not classify censing as a ceremony at all. His reductio is the ceremonially-carried silk veil over the vessels at St Paul's: *"This cannot be called an additional ceremony… the use of Incense is identical in kind with such added details, and has the further justification, which they lack, of being covered by the Ornaments Rubric."* ⚠️ **Concedes: "The language of the rubric is loosely constructed"; that the "times of their ministration" limitation should be extended to the ornaments of the Church even though "These words are not grammatically connected" with them; that "a censer might be used in such a way as to involve an additional ceremony… Such cases would undoubtedly call for the intervention of authority"; that on the approach to the altar "It would be idle to contend that it compares with them in point of conspicuousness"; and he volunteers a ground for curtailing his own practice — the elaborate Sarum censing of all the assistants "may be urged as a ground for a considerable simplification of the ancient details."** ⛔ **And Lacey never names *Martin v. Mackonochie*. He answers the additional-ceremony doctrine in the abstract; the case that created it is handled only in the main Statement.**

**Appendix J — T. A. Lacey, *Incense under the Prayer Book of 1549* (pp. 171–173; the heading OCRs as `I.`).** The short closing appendix, and it is a **pure argument from enumerated silence**. Lacey prints the Articles of c. 1549–50 on which Ridley's Injunctions were founded, whose second Article forbids by name a long catalogue of *"popish"* acts — kissing the Lord's table, washing fingers, blessing the eyes with the paten, shifting the book, licking the chalice, breathing on the bread, elevation, ringing of sacring bells, setting any light on the Lord's board — ⭐ **and incense is in none of them.** *"It is inconceivable that so conspicuous an act should have been left unmentioned if it were regarded as forbidden."* He repeats the argument on the ninth Article's *"strange jumble"* (purgatory, images, relics, lights, holy water, palms, ashes, candles, sepulchres, creeping to the cross, oil, chrism, altars) and on Ridley's 1550 Visitation Articles, which ask about hallowing water, bread, salt, bells, candles, ashes, palms and the font: *"Observe the absence of any question about the use of Incense. It was more likely to be retained than many of the above things; yet it is not 'enquired of' as a thing unlawful."* ⛔⛔ **And then he prints the counter-evidence against himself, which is the third such moment in the book and the frankest:** Ridley's own Injunction restrains the minister to *"only the ceremonies **and gestures** appointed by the Book of Common Prayer, and none other"* — and Lacey writes: ***"Strictly interpreted this would exclude the use of Incense. It is not impossible that Ridley, while shrinking from the express condemnation of Incense, intended in this way, by a side wind, to get rid of it."*** His answer is that Ridley's gesture-prohibition contradicts the Prayer Book's own *Certain Notes* (*"kneeling, crossing, holding up of hands… may be used or left, as every man's devotion serveth, without blame"*) — ⚠️ **a real rejoinder, but an argument, not a refutation.** His stated conclusion is deliberately modest: *"That the Reformers, even the more extreme, under the Prayer Book of 1549, carefully avoided any express prohibition of Incense, **even if they secretly desired to put it out of use.**"*

## 3. *Directorium Anglicanum* and the handbooks

The practical expression of the case was the ceremonial manual. The principal one is **John Purchas, *Directorium Anglicanum: being a Manual of Directions for the right Celebration of the Holy Communion, for the Saying of Matins and Evensong, and for the Performance of the other Rites and Ceremonies of the Church*, London, 1858** — the standard work on Anglican ritualism, later editions edited by Frederick George Lee.

Its governing premise is the same as the submission's: the Prayer Book's rubrics presuppose a body of ceremonial knowledge they do not spell out, and the nineteenth-century priest lacks it. The book's stated object was to put "the Priest of the nineteenth century on a par with the Priest of the sixteenth century as to ritual knowledge" — i.e. to supply the **assumed** ceremonial the rubrics are silent about. ⭐ **That is §2d(i) turned into a practical handbook: if silence is permissive because the rubrics were never a complete code, then the missing directions must be recovered from the old English uses, and the *Directorium* is the recovery.**

Incense was one of the "six points" the ritualists pressed (with eucharistic vestments, the eastward position, altar lights, the mixed chalice and wafer bread).

⚠️ **The book's author is the same John Purchas who lost the leading case.** That is not a coincidence but it is worth stating precisely: he wrote the manual in 1858 and was prosecuted for doing what it prescribed a decade later (§4b).

## 4. The legal history, 1868–1890

### 4a. *Martin v. Mackonochie* (Court of Arches 1868; Privy Council 1868–9)

Sir Robert Phillimore, Dean of the Arches, upheld some complaints against A. H. Mackonochie of St Alban's, Holborn and dismissed others. The Privy Council overruled him on the dismissals. ⭐ **The doctrine that mattered came from the Privy Council's reading of the Prayer Book Preface *Of Ceremonies*:** the Preface "divides all ceremonies into 2 classes: those which are retained are specified… none are abolished specifically… but it is assumed that all are abolished which are not expressly retained."

⚠️ **That single sentence is the ritualists' central obstacle, and everything in §2b is built to get around it.**

Phillimore nonetheless said of incense that "It certainly was in use in the Church of England in the time of King Edward the Sixth's First Prayer Book" — a concession the 1899 submission quotes and leans on, while arguing that the censer-as-ornament point "was not made before him."

### 4b. *Elphinstone v. Purchas* (Arches, 3 February 1870) and *Hebbert v. Purchas* (Privy Council, 1871)

John Purchas, perpetual curate of St James's Chapel, Brighton, was charged in November 1869 with a long list of ritualist practices including incense. He did not appear, pleading poverty and ill health. Phillimore decided against him on 3 February 1870 — but in terms that did not satisfy the prosecutor, **Phillimore having upheld eucharistic vestments and other points**. Elphinstone appealed; he died before the appeal was heard, and Henry Hebbert was substituted. The Judicial Committee, hearing argument across November–December 1870 and February–April 1871, went against Purchas **on all points**, holding eucharistic vestments, the eastward position, the mixed chalice and wafer bread illegal.

⚠️ **Two corrections to the way this case is commonly cited, including in this project's own `RJ_Incense_Analysis.md` §8:**

1. **The case name and date do not travel together.** *Elphinstone v. Purchas* is the **Arches** case of **1870**; *Hebbert v. Purchas* is the **Privy Council** appeal of **1871**. Citing "Elphinstone v. Purchas (1870)" for the incense holding is correct as to the Arches; citing "the Purchas judgment (1871)" means the Privy Council. They are different courts and different decisions, and Phillimore was **more** favourable to the ritualists than the Board that reversed him.
2. **Purchas did not comply.** He was suspended for twelve months, could not be made to pay costs (having put his property out of his hands), continued his services until his death in 1872, and the decision "caused a controversy which extended over a considerable period."

⭐ **The legal mechanism by which the Ornaments Rubric argument was defeated is the "further order" proviso of the 1559 Act.** *Hebbert v. Purchas*, and then *Ridsdale v. Clifton* (Privy Council, May 1877), held that Archbishop Parker's **Advertisements of 1566** constituted the "other order" the proviso contemplated, so that the rubric's reach was cut back to the surplice (with the cope in cathedral and collegiate churches). ⚠️ **This is the hinge of the entire legal defeat, and it is worth noting that the 1899 ritualist submission NEVER MENTIONS the Advertisements, never asks whether "further order" was taken, and never cites *Hebbert v. Purchas* at all.** (Verified by search of the retrieved portion. The argument may have sat in Geldart's Appendix D or Frere's Appendix E, neither retrieved — so this is an observed silence in the text read, not a demonstrated omission from the case as a whole.)

### 4c. The Public Worship Regulation Act 1874

Introduced as a private member's bill in the Lords by **Archbishop Tait on 20 April 1874**; royal assent **7 August 1874**; commenced 1 July 1875. Disraeli backed it as "a bill to put down ritualism" and called Anglo-Catholic practice "a Mass in masquerade"; Queen Victoria supported it; Gladstone opposed it, disgusted that the liturgy was made "a parliamentary football."

Mechanically: s. 8 let an archdeacon, churchwarden, or three adult male parishioners serve a representation on the bishop; **the bishop had discretion to stay proceedings**; if he did not, a new court under Lord Penzance (a former Divorce Court judge) heard the case.

⭐⭐ **Its effect was the opposite of what was intended.** Five ritualist priests were imprisoned for contempt — **Arthur Tooth** (St James's, Hatcham, 1877), **T. Pelham Dale** (St Vedast Foster Lane, 1880), **Richard Enraght** (Holy Trinity, Bordesley, 1880), **Sidney Faithorn Green** (St John's, Miles Platting, 1881–82, over eighteen months), and **James Bell Cox** (St Margaret's, Liverpool, 1887). The imprisonments discredited the Act and the prosecutions; the bishops' discretionary veto was increasingly used to shield ritualist clergy. Prosecutions effectively ended after the 1906 Royal Commission. The Act was not repealed until 1 March 1965.

⚠️ **The martyrdom effect is a real historical fact, not ritualist rhetoric, and it is part of why the 1899 Opinion took the form of an *opinion* rather than a prosecution.**

### 4d. *Read v. Bishop of Lincoln* (1890) — the Lincoln Judgment

The Church Association complained of Bishop Edward King of Lincoln's ritual. There being no statutory procedure for trying a bishop, **Archbishop E. W. Benson tried the case himself** under the common-law metropolitical jurisdiction, delivering judgment on **21 November 1890**.

Benson found substantially for King. He defined a **ceremony** as "a formal symbolic gesture of religious meaning publicly made by the Minister in his character of Minister, rendering the delivery of language more solemn, and not merely expressing his private devotion" — a definition that distinguishes a ceremony (an action) from a rite (words) and an ornament (a thing). He held lawful: the **eastward position**; the **mixed chalice if mixed before the service** (the act of mixing during service being an added ceremony, and so unlawful); the **Agnus Dei**; and **two lighted candles on the Holy Table**, which he treated as **ornaments** rather than as ceremony — directly contradicting the Privy Council in *Mackonochie*. He held unlawful the **sign of the cross in Absolution and Benediction**, on the ground that they were post-Reformation importations rather than revived mediaeval usage, and required the manual acts to be visible to the communicants.

⭐ **Why the Lincoln Judgment matters for incense.** It did **not** rule on incense. But it did three things the ritualists valued and the 1899 submission cites: it treated a disputed item as an **ornament** rather than a ceremony (exactly the move of §2b); it accepted a **catena of historical usage** as evidence, which the submission cites explicitly — "The value of such a catena was recognized in the Lincoln judgment as evidence in favour of Altar Lights"; and it decided the case on the Prayer Book and history rather than on Privy Council precedent.

⚠️ **The Privy Council let the Lincoln Judgment stand on a narrow technicality** — that King had not lit the candles himself and approval alone was not an offence — expressly declining to endorse Benson's reasoning on candles. **The precedent is therefore weaker than its reputation.**

## 5. The 1899 Opinion in its own context

### 5a. What it procedurally was

**Not a court.** The Bishop of London referred the practices of the Rev. H. Westall of St Cuthbert's, Philbeach Gardens, to the Archbishop under the Prayer Book Preface *Concerning the Service of the Church*, which directs a doubting minister to "resort to the Bishop of the Diocese, who by his discretion shall take order for the quieting and appeasing of the same; so that the same order be not contrary to any thing contained in this Book. And if the Bishop of the Diocese be in doubt, then he may send for the resolution thereof to the Archbishop."

The hearing was at Lambeth on **Monday 8 May 1899**; the Opinion was delivered at Lambeth Palace on **31 July 1899**, by **Archbishop Frederick Temple of Canterbury**, with **Archbishop William Maclagan of York** sitting with him.

⚠️⚠️ **The ritualists entered their jurisdictional objection BEFORE the hearing, not after losing it.** Westall's submission opens:

> "I desire to state at the outset, as was done by the Bishop of Lincoln when formally cited to appear before the late Archbishop of Canterbury… (1) that the only authority which I can recognize as competent finally to determine and settle such matters is the Synod of the Province, and (2) that in thus appearing before your Grace I base my conduct, not on a particular clause in the Preface of the Prayer Book which I believe can be shown to refer to a totally different class of matters, but on the obedience due to the inherent authority of my Bishop, and to that of your Grace, in so far as your Grace represents the authority of the Synod of the Province."

⛔ **This matters enormously for §6.** The ground on which the ritualists later refused to be bound was **pre-registered, in writing, at the outset of the proceeding.** It was not invented after an adverse result.

Note also what Westall actually asked for:

> "In view of the long disuse, I do not ask for any pronouncement which should enforce the use of Incense or Processional Lights on others… but I would ask for a statement to the effect that I am breaking no law of the Church."

### 5b. What the Archbishops actually ruled, and on what reasoning

The Opinion frames the question exactly as the ritualists had: "There is no direction in the Book of Common Prayer either enjoining or authorising either of these practices. But it is argued that they are practically sanctioned by the Ornaments Rubric… And it is consequently this Rubric that we have to interpret."

**The reasoning has five moves.**

1. **The two limitations.** The rubric's ornaments are limited by *"at all Times of their Ministration"* and by *"the second year."* An ornament failing either is outside the rubric altogether.
2. **"Ministration" means what the Prayer Book prescribes.** Canon 36 binds every clergyman to "use the Form in the said Book prescribed and none other," which "prevents the insertion of any additional prayer or ceremony without special authority." The only authorities that can vary it are an Act of Convocation, the Crown with the Metropolitan's advice under the 1559 Act, or the Ordinary under the Act of 1872.
3. **Prohibition, not merely omission.** They decline to decide whether omission is prohibition, and rest instead on express words: "Whether omission is prohibition it is not necessary for us to consider. But prohibition is prohibition" — pointing to the 1559 Act's "none other or otherwise" and its avoidance of all other forms.
4. **⭐⭐ The decisive move — the inversion argument.** This is the direct answer to §2b, and it is the strongest thing in the Opinion:

> "The Rubric is often carelessly interpreted as if the order to use the Ornaments was 'per se' an order to use the appropriate ceremonies. But, in the first place, this interpretation makes the Act of Elizabeth contradict itself by ordering a strict adherence to the form of ceremonial prescribed in the Book, and at the same time ordering the revival of a number of ceremonies of which the Book says nothing. In the second place, it inverts the relation between a ceremony and an ornament, by making the ceremony subordinate to the ornament, instead of the ornament being subordinate to the ceremony. The very meaning of an ornament is that it is a thing to be used for the fitting performance of a ceremony, and if no ceremony be prescribed the so-called ornament has no place. In the third place, to order a ceremony not directly, but by ordering the use of an ornament connected with it, is without any precedent in the history of the Church. And lastly, this interpretation makes the words 'at all times of their ministrations' not merely unmeaning but misleading."

5. **The date question is left open.** Having decided on the first limitation, they expressly decline the second: "Whether, as seems most probable, the authority of Parliament in the second year… means the first Act of Uniformity, and therefore the Prayer Book of 1549, or means the immediately preceding state of the law, does not affect the arguments which we have used."

**On the historical evidence** they concede more than is often noticed: they accept that the 1559 Act "was imperfectly obeyed," that Sandys "goes so far as to say that he interprets the Act as one that would not be really enforced," and that "there seems to have been a certain amount of conscious and still more unconscious disobedience." Their answer is the **liturgical/fumigatory distinction** — that the instances produced at the hearing were fumigatory, that George Herbert "used incense to sweeten his Church. There is no liturgical use in this," and that when those are struck out "the number of cases in which incense was used in worship is utterly insufficient."

**On why the Reformers dropped it**, three reasons: the desire for greater simplicity; the desire to conform the Communion office to the New Testament account of the institution; and the belief that incense "was certainly not in use in the Church for at least three hundred years from the Apostolic times." ⚠️ **The third reason is the one Frere had already conceded (§2g) — so on this point the two sides agree, and the disagreement is about what follows from it.**

**Two things the Opinion is careful NOT to say.** First, that incense is bad: "the Church has never spoken of incense as an evil thing," the Homilies being "hortatory rather than imperative" and never taken as "having high authority on points of doctrine or of ritual"; and "we are far from saying that incense in itself is an unsuitable or undesirable accompaniment to Divine worship. The injunction for its use by Divine authority in the Jewish Church would alone forbid such a conclusion." Second, that it is permanently excluded: "even now the liturgical use of incense is not by law permanently excluded from the Church's Ritual" — the Crown with the Primate's consent could order it in future.

**The holding:**

> "…the use of incense in the public worship, and as a part of that worship, is not at present enjoined nor permitted by the law of the Church of England; and it is our duty to request the Clergy who so use it to discontinue that use. If used at all, it must be used (in George Herbert's language) to sweeten the Church, and outside the worship altogether."

The same reasoning was applied to processional lights, "and in this decision we have the support of the late Archbishop Benson in his judgment in the case of the Bishop of Lincoln."

### 5c. What authority it carried

⭐⭐⭐ **By Temple's own account in the House of Lords, 16 July 1900, none that could be enforced:**

> "I know the Prayer-book gives me no power to coerce. I know perfectly well that the Prayer-book does not constitute the Archbishop or the two Archbishops a court with power to punish. I know all I can do is simply to declare what, in my opinion… is within the Church's law or outside it."

⚠️ **Both sides in that debate further conceded that the Opinion bound a clergyman only where his own diocesan took it up.** The Earl of Portsmouth, attacking the Archbishops, quoted Temple's own reply to a deputation — that a priest whose "bishop does not call upon me to obey this opinion, therefore I am not bound by it" — precisely because it "entirely and absolutely gives away the whole case."

**Its actual force was moral and episcopal, and it was exercised through the bishops.** In **1901 every bishop except Sodor and Man signed a joint letter**: "We therefore put before you that we as a body uphold the duty of submitting to the decision of the Archbishops lately given on questions referred to them, in accordance with the direction in the Book of Common Prayer."

## 6. How the ritualists responded — their STATED grounds for continuing

⭐⭐⭐ **This section is the reason the pass was worth running.** The corpus already records that many clergy simply continued. What it did not hold is **why they said they were entitled to.** The Royal Commission of 1906 printed the documents.

### 6a. The London incumbents' protest, October 1899

Bishop Creighton of London told his clergy that "It becomes a universal duty to abandon the ceremonial use of incense and the use of lights carried about during the service." **About twenty-five incumbents presented him with a protest**, presented at an interview on 16 October 1899. Verbatim:

> "On the one hand our duty compels us even at the cost of great distress to our people to yield whatever compliance we can to the expressed wish of our Father in God. On the other hand we dare not abandon altogether 'a laudable practice of the whole Catholic Church of Christ,' conspicuously scriptural and hitherto held to be sanctioned by the Ornaments Rubric of the Book of Common Prayer. We beg respectfully to remind your Lordship that the use of incense was unquestionably practised by Bishop Andrewes, and has been revived among us for more than forty years with (to say the least) the acquiescence of Bishops, notably that of your Lordship's predecessor in the see of London. We would explain that for these and other reasons we are obliged to regard that use of incense as involving a principle, the violation of which we could neither submit to ourselves nor ask our people to accept. Moreover, we find ourselves utterly unable to do anything by which we may be held, either explicitly or implicitly, to admit the binding authority of the Archbishops' Opinion or the force of the reasons on which that opinion is based."

**Four distinct grounds, and they should be counted separately:**

1. **Catholic obligation** — "a laudable practice of the whole Catholic Church of Christ," a phrase deliberately lifted from the Prayer Book's own Preface *Of Ceremonies*.
2. **Scriptural warrant** — "conspicuously scriptural."
3. **The Ornaments Rubric** — "hitherto held to be sanctioned by the Ornaments Rubric."
4. **Acquiescence and prescription** — revived "for more than forty years with… the acquiescence of Bishops."

And then the flat refusal of the Opinion's authority *and* of its reasoning, stated as two separate denials.

### 6b. The compromise, and what it conceded

Having refused the Opinion, they yielded to their bishop personally. Their letter of 30 October 1899:

> "…we are accordingly prepared to modify our practice by suspending for the present in our several churches the customary ceremonial use of incense, by which we understand the censing of persons and things during the service as set out in the Prayer Book. But we beg to remind your Lordship that such a modification of the accustomed use is made **without any reference on our part to the Archbishops' Opinion, the binding authority of which we have felt it our duty respectfully to deny, and the reasons of which as stated we have ventured… definitely to repudiate.** We would explain that we make this alteration **solely in compliance with the wish of our Diocesan** and in dutiful regard for his person and office and for the good of the Church."

⭐ **The structure is exact and it is the live position:** obedience to the *person* of the bishop, coupled with express denial that the *ruling* binds. The Rev. W. B. Trevelyan, their spokesman, then wrote again on 2 November to pin down that the concession covered only "during the service as set out in the Book of Common Prayer" — preserving censing before the service and in processions. **Creighton did not reply.**

The resulting practice was: the censer used before the Communion service and in processions before or after services; and "a swinging of the censer during the service for the purpose of keeping it alight, but not for the purpose of censing persons or things."

⚠️⚠️ **The Royal Commission itself pointed out that the compromise did not meet the law even as Temple had stated it.** Phillimore had already held that processions immediately before or after a service, before the same congregation, are bound by the same restrictions; and Temple wrote to the Bishop of Winchester on 28 October 1899: "A procession with incense is clearly an additional ceremony not ordered in the Book of Common Prayer… According to our present law, incense cannot be used in any way to be a part of public worship."

### 6c. What became of Westall

⭐ **The man who argued the case at Lambeth got a private accommodation.** Shortly before his death in December 1900, Bishop Creighton made a special arrangement with **two** churches — **St Cuthbert's, Philbeach Gardens** (Westall's own) and **St Alban's, Holborn** — "that these churches should altogether suspend their use of incense on ordinary occasions, but that on great festivals they should be allowed their full accustomed use." No definition of "great festivals" was attempted, and the Commission records that the phrase was construed by some clergy "with such latitude as to include All Souls' Day."

### 6d. The numbers

- **1906 Royal Commission:** incense used **ceremonially in 99** and **non-ceremonially in 10** of the **559** churches reported on — "It is doubtless used in many more." The *Tourist's Church Guide* for **1902** gave **393** for England and Wales.
- **Diocesan distribution of the 99:** London 29, York 10, Exeter 7, St Albans 7, Southwark 6, Norwich 5, Oxford 5, Birmingham 4, then smaller numbers elsewhere.
- **London, May 1901** (Bishop of London's evidence): about **40** churches using incense — ten for thirty years or more, six for twenty, ten for ten, thirteen for five, one introduced within five years. **No increase between May 1901 and May 1905.**
- **Outcome of Creighton's action:** of the churches where incense was customary, **2** abandoned it altogether, **25** modified in line with one or other compromise, **6** altered slightly or resumed during the vacancy of the see, and **8** made no change at all.
- **Six London churches** were placed under "discipline" — no episcopal visitation, non-renewal of curates' licences, withdrawal of curates' grants: St Peter's London Docks; St Mary Magdalene's Paddington; St Mary's Edmonton; St Augustine's Stepney; St Michael's North Kensington; St Clement's City Road.

⚠️ **The Bishop of London's own account of why he did not prosecute is worth having verbatim, because it is the mechanism of "legalisation by attrition" stated by the man doing it:**

> "I made up my mind that to prosecute these forty-three churches would make it impossible to do my work as bishop at all, that it would put the whole diocese in a flame… Then the third alternative, of course, was to continue the arrangement which I found existing… the toleration of incense, not the sanction of it, but the toleration of it."

### 6e. The wider grounds, from the 1900 Lords debate

Beyond the London incumbents, two further grounds are on record in the debate of 16 July 1900:

- **A lay memorial:** "We protest against your Grace's attempt to foist upon the Church a penal Act of Parliament passed in days of regal autocracy… upon the principles of Catholic law, and not upon the construction of the alleged law of the State."
- **Lord Halifax**, president of the English Church Union, in the first person: "unable to recognise the competency of a court like the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council to declare the doctrine and the ritual of the Church of England."
- **Temple's own characterisation of the disobeyers' ground:** "the belief that practices which they have pursued are really within the law of the Church."

⚠️ **Reported honestly:** the "only a provincial Synod can settle this" ground, which Westall pre-registered in writing in May 1899 (§5a), is **not** found in the 1900 Lords debate. Halifax's nearest statement — "The Bishops and the synods are the real guardians of the faith" — is a limiting move about episcopal competence to construe Acts of Parliament, not a demand for synodical determination. **Two related but distinct grounds; do not merge them.**

⚠️ **Also reported honestly:** the frequently repeated figure of "25 London churches using incense after the Opinion" traces to the Earl of Portsmouth in that debate — "In London to-day it is used in twenty-five churches" — offered with **no baseline**, alongside a rhetorical "just as it was before." The number 25 also appears in the same speech meaning something entirely different (churches with midday communions and no communicant but the priest). **The reliable figures are the Royal Commission's, in §6d, and the "25" that matters is the number of London incumbents who signed the protest and the number who adopted a compromise — which is a different 25 again.**

## 7. The honest assessment

⛔⛔ **Stated in both directions, and the strong half first. A case rehearsed only in its weak form is worthless for anticipating what an interlocutor will actually say.**

### 7a. Where the ritualist case has real force

**1. The "silence is permissive" argument is not a dodge — it is a genuine problem for the strict view, and its reductio has never been squarely answered.** The submission's list at §2d(iii) is devastating in its ordinariness: coloured altar cloths, the credence table, turning east for the Creed, "Glory be to Thee, O Lord" before the Gospel, hymns. **Every one of these is unauthorised by the letter of the Prayer Book, and every one of them was and is universally practised, including by the strictest opponents of ritualism.** The 1899 Opinion concedes the point in principle — it acknowledges that the responses before and after the Gospel, the people saying the General Thanksgiving aloud, and shortening the Exhortation are "probably in strictness all illegal" — and then draws the line at practices "conspicuous, not sanctioned by long-continued custom in our Church, and of such a nature as to change the general character and aspect of the service." ⚠️ **That is a real distinction, but it is a distinction of degree, applied by judgement, not a rule derived from the text. The ritualists are entitled to say so, and they did.**

**2. The ornament/ceremony asymmetry is textually real.** The Preface *Of Ceremonies* is subtitled "why some be abolished, and some retained," which supports a closed-list reading for ceremonies. **The Ornaments Rubric contains no comparable language.** On its wording it *requires* certain ornaments; it does not on its face *forbid* others. Benson's Lincoln Judgment treated candles as ornaments rather than ceremony and reached a ritualist-friendly result by doing so — and the Privy Council, given the chance to overrule him, declined and disposed of the appeal on a technicality instead. ⭐ **So the ritualist classification move is not eccentric: an Archbishop of Canterbury made it in a reasoned judgment, and the Privy Council flinched from correcting him.**

**3. The historical evidence of survival is better than a bare "three centuries of disuse" narrative allows.** 27% of 1,402 inventoried churches retaining censers, against 3% recorded as sold; the Bodmin indenture of 1566 committing a censer to the churchwardens "to be used and occupied"; the Poole inventory of 1559 listing censers in a parish certified as conforming to the Prayer Book and Injunctions; Cosin's use and his approval of ceremonial censing; the Ely bills. ⚠️ **This does not establish continuous ceremonial use — see §7b(3) — but it does establish that the object did not vanish and that its retention was not everywhere thought unlawful. A claim that censers were simply swept away in 1552 is too strong.**

**4. The 1899 Opinion's authority is genuinely thin, and the ritualists were right about that as a matter of law.** It was not a court. Temple said so himself, unprompted, in the Lords. It carried no power to coerce, bound a clergyman only where his diocesan took it up, and Temple conceded in terms that a priest whose bishop did not call on him to obey was not bound. ⛔ **Any argument that treats the 1899 Opinion as a binding judicial determination is overclaiming, and an informed Anglo-Catholic will say so immediately.** Its weight is that of a considered interpretation by the highest interpretative authority the Prayer Book's own machinery provides — which is considerable, but is not the same thing.

**5. The tu quoque about episcopal ornaments has never been answered.** If the Ornaments Rubric does not legalise ornaments beyond those named in the 1549 Book, then the mitre, the pastoral staff and the primatial cross borne before the Archbishops themselves are without authority. ⚠️ **The 1899 Opinion does not address this. It is not a knockdown argument, but it is a real inconsistency in the position of the tribunal that decided the case.**

**6. Their patristic concession removes the easiest refutation.** Frere conceded, in the ritualists' own submission, that liturgical incense is unattested in the first three centuries. ⛔ **Anyone who "refutes" the ritualist case by producing that fact is producing the ritualists' own expert's finding and has not touched the argument, which is legal and does not depend on patristic antiquity.**

### 7b. Where the ritualist case is weak

**1. The inversion argument is, in this reader's judgement, the strongest single point on either side, and the submission has no good answer to it.** An ornament is a thing used for the performance of a ceremony. If the ceremony is not prescribed, the ornament has nothing to attach to. To order a ceremony *indirectly*, by ordering the use of an implement associated with it, is — as the Archbishops said — "without any precedent in the history of the Church." ⚠️ **The submission's answer is the "accustomed use travels with the ornament" claim at §2b, which simply asserts what the Archbishops deny, and its alternative plea (not a ceremony at all, merely "a subsidiary detail") strains: censing persons and things at four defined points in the liturgy is plainly a formal symbolic gesture publicly made — which is Benson's own definition of a ceremony, from the judgment the ritualists were citing in their favour.**

**2. The "at all Times of their Ministration" limitation is textual and the submission underweights it.** The rubric covers ornaments used *at all times of ministration*. A censer is not used at all times of ministration on anyone's account. The Archbishops' point that the ritualist reading makes those words "not merely unmeaning but misleading" is a straightforward canon of construction, and the submission's two-filter reading (§2a) is an ingenious reconstruction of the clause rather than a natural reading of it.

**3. The survival evidence proves possession, not ceremonial use — and the pleaders knew it.** Inventories list objects. The submission's own catena is thin at exactly the point where it needs to be thick: the Chapel Royal Epiphany offering is introduced as "by no means the strongest piece of evidence"; Andrewes' censer is in a **private chapel** with no source given; George Herbert and Durham are conceded as fumigatory; the Ely dates are internally corrupt as printed. ⛔ **The pleaders themselves concede that irregular uses "cannot be legally justified" and offer them only as negative evidence. That is honest, but it means the affirmative case for continuous ceremonial use rests almost entirely on appendices whose contents this document has not read — so the strength of that case is, for present purposes, UNVERIFIED rather than refuted.**

**4. The silence on the "further order" proviso is the case's largest gap.** The 1559 Act's ornaments clause was expressly temporary — "until other order shall be therein taken." *Hebbert v. Purchas* and *Ridsdale v. Clifton* held that Parker's Advertisements of 1566 were that other order. **The retrieved portion of the submission never mentions the Advertisements, never asks whether other order was taken, and never cites *Purchas*.** ⚠️ **Stated with the caveat it deserves: the argument may live in an unretrieved appendix. But on the text read, the case is silent at precisely the point where the courts had already decided against it.**

**5. Disuse is not nothing, whatever the desuetude doctrine says.** The submission is right that a statute does not lose validity by disuse. But it concedes the practice "well nigh totally disappeared" and survived only "in a sporadic and unauthorised manner." ⭐ **A claim to be restoring the Church of England's own accustomed ceremonial sits awkwardly with a gap of two centuries or more in which nobody in the Church of England was doing it — and the ritualists needed a nineteenth-century handbook (§3) to tell nineteenth-century priests how.**

**6. The 1899 Opinion's core historical reading has never been answered.** The Prayer Book's Preface says of ceremonies "why some be abolished, and some retained." The Privy Council's gloss — none are abolished specifically, but it is assumed all are abolished which are not expressly retained — is a natural reading of that heading. ⛔ **What happened after 1899 was not that this reading was refuted. It was that the machinery for enforcing it was dismantled around a practice that continued regardless.**

### 7c. The one thing both sides agreed on

Liturgical incense was **not** in use in the primitive Church. Frere conceded it for the ritualists; the Archbishops gave it as their third reason for the Reformers' omission. ⚠️ **Any argument built on "the early Church censed" is contradicted by the ritualists' own expert and should not be advanced.**

### 7d. ⭐⭐⭐ The assessment revised on the full evidentiary case (dated addition, 260835-34)

> ⛔⛔ **§§7a–7c stand unaltered, per never-alter. This is not an appendix of extra examples — the brief required a judgement on whether the assessment ITSELF changes now that Appendices A–J have been read, and it does. ⭐ The headline is that §7 graded the case as a single thing, and the full material shows it is TWO cases of unequal strength that §7's structure ran together.**
>
> ### ⭐ The verdict, stated plainly first
>
> **The LEGAL and CONSTRUCTIONAL arm is materially STRONGER than §7a allows — stronger than this file previously had grounds to say.**
> **The EVIDENTIARY arm — continuous ceremonial use after the Reformation — is NOT stronger. §7b(3) called it "UNVERIFIED rather than refuted"; it is now verified, and it comes out substantially where §7b(3) guessed it would.** ⛔ **On that arm the appendices do not rescue the case, and the reason they do not is that the ritualists' own experts had already declined to claim what a modern advocate would want to claim for them.**
>
> ### (1) ⭐⭐⭐ Three genuine strengthenings, none of which §7a had
>
> **(a) The "silence is permissive" argument is much stronger than §2d(ii)'s three prohibitory rubrics made it look — because of Appendix J.** §7a(1) rated the reductio as the case's best point. It is now not the best point. **Lacey shows that the Edwardine Articles of c. 1549–50, and Ridley's Visitation Articles of 1550, enumerate the forbidden "popish" acts by name — some twenty of them, including sacring bells, elevation, lights on the Lord's board, holy water, palms, ashes, candles, creeping to the cross, oil, chrism and altars — and incense is in none of the lists.** ⭐ **That is not bare silence; it is silence inside what is on its face an exhaustive enumeration of the very category, drawn up by the men most hostile to what it enumerates.** The Archbishops' answer at §5b(3) — *"Whether omission is prohibition it is not necessary for us to consider. But prohibition is prohibition"* — is a good answer to bare omission. It is a **markedly weaker** answer to a specific, itemised, contemporaneous prohibitory list that goes out of its way to name lesser things and passes over incense. ⛔ **Recorded with its limit: Lacey himself prints the counter-evidence (Ridley's "ceremonies and gestures… and none other") and concedes that "Strictly interpreted this would exclude the use of Incense."**
>
> **(b) The custom argument now has an actual legal precedent, which §7 did not know about.** *Eliz. Shipden v. Dr. Redman* (Appendix E): a prohibition sought against an ecclesiastical order on the express plea that it *"was not warranted by any Custom or Canon of the Church of England"*; the temporal judges referred the question to the Archbishop, the bishops certified the practice *"the ancient usage of the Church of England,"* and *"a prohibition was denied."* ⭐ **§7a(2) said the ritualist classification move "is not eccentric" because Benson made it. The stronger point is now available: a secular court has once deferred to episcopal certification of unwritten ancient usage against precisely the argument the Archbishops deployed in 1899.** ⚠️ **Discounted as it should be: it concerns a garment at a churching, not an ornament in a prescribed rite, and Frere does not address the disanalogy.**
>
> **(c) One documentary datum of real ceremonial weight that §2f did not have.** The **St Michael, Cornhill vestry order of 24 August 1589** — *"ffyre at all such ffeastes as Incense is accustomed to be offered unto Allmightie God… according to the solempnitie of the ffeaste"* — deliberately re-adopted and minuted *"for perpetual memory."* ⭐ **A London parish, thirty years after the settlement, recording incense offered to God at feasts as a standing obligation is a materially better instance than anything §2f held.** ⚠️ **Its limit, which Frere states: the wording is Henrician; 1589 is the re-adoption, not the drafting.** Add **Solihull, 1665**, buying frankincense *"for the first Sacrament"* alongside the bread and wine, and **Ely's account books**, where the Dean's own 1899 letter evidences regular twice-yearly purchases of frankincense from **1708 to 1747**.
>
> ### (2) ⛔⛔ Where the full material makes the case WEAKER, or confirms §7b
>
> **(a) §7b(3) is CONFIRMED, and by the best possible authority — the pleaders' own classification.** The appendices do not establish continuous ceremonial use. Frere's own catena in Appendix H is, on his own express account, mostly about smell: he prints the churchwardens' entries and then writes that George Herbert *"explains the foregoing entries."* ⛔⛔ **The 1899 Opinion's most-criticised evidentiary move — sorting the instances into liturgical and fumigatory and striking out the latter — turns out to be the sorting Frere had already performed on his own material. The Archbishops did not impose an alien distinction on the ritualists' evidence; they adopted the ritualists' expert's own.** ⭐ **This should change how the 1899 Opinion is characterised. It is a good deal fairer to the submission than a reader of §5b alone would think.**
>
> **(b) The best late instance is disclaimed by the man producing it.** §7b(3) called the Ely dates "internally corrupt as printed." That understates and slightly misdescribes: **Frere identifies the incompatibility himself, discusses it, and then declines to claim the conclusion** — *"the statement that Incense was swung ceremonially by the Deacon at the Altar **awaits further confirmation**."* ⚠️ **So the purchases at Ely are documentary and certain; the ceremonial swinging is testimony at two removes which the ritualists' own historian would not assert.**
>
> **(c) ⛔⛔ Geldart concedes the extinction, and dates it — and this is worse for the case than §7b(5) said.** §7b(5) noted the practice "well nigh totally disappeared." Appendix D is more specific and more damaging: the 1552 silence about incense reflects *"**the fact of all such things (or nearly all) having ceased between 1550 and 1552.**"* ⭐ **The ritualists' own rubrical expert dates the practical extinction of incense to within three years of the second year of Edward VI. — the very moment on which the entire legal claim is anchored.** That does not defeat the legal argument, which is about lawfulness and not about practice, but it removes any suggestion of a living tradition to be *continued* rather than *revived*, and it is the ritualists themselves who remove it.
>
> **(d) The confiscation statistic carries a limit the Statement's summary concealed.** The 27% is real and the table is arithmetically sound (§2f-bis). ⛔ **But Hope prints the commission of 16 January 1552-3 that ordered the sale of all remaining metal and ornaments; the inventories were taken *before* it. The figure measures what was there to be taken, not what survived.**
>
> **(e) §7b(4) is not merely confirmed but sharpened, and it is now the case's largest defect beyond argument.** The Advertisements are named nowhere in 329,971 bytes; *Purchas* and *Ridsdale* are cited nowhere. ⛔ **And the submission quotes the "until other order" proviso twice while never asking whether other order was taken** (§4b dated note). ⚠️ **§7b(4)'s protective caveat — "the argument may live in an unretrieved appendix" — is FALSIFIED and must not be repeated.**
>
> **(f) The patristic concession is broader than §7c records.** §7c states that liturgical incense was not in use in the primitive Church. Frere concedes more than that: that the Fathers positively *disprove* it *"so far as they go"*; that Levitical precedent would have told *against* the use in the early Church, not for it; that ceremonial traces are scarce even after Constantine; that **liturgical-book evidence "only seriously begins at the close of the seventh, or the opening of the eighth century"**; and that altar-censing specifically is *"one of the later forms."* ⭐ **Darwell Stone concedes the same thing independently in Appendix C. §7c should be read as understating a concession made twice.**
>
> ### (3) ⭐⭐ The finding that is about the document rather than the argument
>
> **In seven of the nine appendices the ritualists' own experts concede material points against their own interest, in print, unprompted, in a submission written to persuade a hostile tribunal.** Frere destroys his own "most crucial passage" from St Ambrose and disowns the puritan testimony that would have helped him; Percival concedes the heathen derivation of incense and names the ground on which a church could lawfully abolish it; Stone concedes it was not used in earliest Christian worship; Geldart concedes that his own marker at the Canon was "not a general use, nor… ordered by old rubrics," that silence is sometimes prohibitive, and that the practice had ceased by 1552; Birkbeck concedes that Eastern practice differs at the points the English want; Lacey concedes that the rubric is "loosely constructed," that a censer *could* be used so as to constitute an additional ceremony, and that Ridley may have meant to suppress incense "by a side wind."
>
> ⛔⛔ **The practical consequence is a warning about how this case should be handled.** Nearly every objection a modern critic would raise against the ritualist position is **already printed inside the ritualist submission, in the mouths of its own experts, usually in stronger terms than an opponent would put it.** ⭐ **An interlocutor who has read the appendices cannot be answered by producing those objections, and will reasonably treat someone who produces them as not having read the case. What the appendices leave genuinely unanswered is narrow and specific: the inversion argument (§7b(1)), the "at all Times of their Ministration" limitation (§7b(2)), and the Advertisements (§7b(4)) — and it is worth noticing that ALL THREE are legal, and NONE of them is historical.**
