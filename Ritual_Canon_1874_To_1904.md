# The 1874 ritual canon and its 1904 repeal

**Occasion.** Pass 260835-60 established that the American ritual canon was
repealed in 1904, by number, and captured the repeal resolution verbatim. It
left two things owed: the numbering trail was unverified end to end, and the
*character* of the repeal was unexamined. JD intends to deploy the repeal in a
public exchange. A targeted removal and a general recodification support very
different claims, and the difference decides whether the word "silently" is
defensible.

## §1. The numbering trail

### What the dated Digests show

Each row is a Digest of the Canons of record, dated by its own title page or by
the Convention whose changes it certifies. Full excerpts and URLs are in the
companion capture file.

| Point | Number | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| 1874, as enacted | Title I, **Canon 20** | moved, amended, conferenced and passed throughout as an added § II to Canon 20 |
| 1874, Digest printed with that Journal | **Canon 22** | "to be added to Canon 20 (renumbered Canon 22) of Title I"; Digest proper prints "22. Of the Use of the Book of Common Prayer" |
| 1880 Journal's Digest | **Canon 22** | index of canons, leaf 729 |
| 1884 Digest | **Canon 22** | index of canons, leaf 169 |
| 1887 Digest (canons through the Convention of 1886) | **Canon 21** | printed p. 89, read off the page: "CANON 20. Of the Due Celebration of Sundays" then "CANON 21. Of the Use of the Book of Common Prayer", with § ii the ritual section |
| 1889 Journal's Digest | **Canon 22** | printed p. 85, read off the page: "CANON 21. Of the Due Celebration of Sundays" then "CANON 22. Of the Use of the Book of Common Prayer" |
| 1896 Digest (through 1895) | **Canon 24** | index of canons, leaf 161 |
| 1901 Journal's Digest | **Canon 24** | canon heading leaf 725; contents leaf 799, "§ ii. — Introduction of Ceremonies or Practices not Ordained or Authorized not Allowed", p. 88 |
| 1904 | **Canon 24, repealed** | Journal 1904, p. 134 |

### Reading

The canon's **title, § i and § ii are continuous and unchanged in substance
across every one of these points.** It was never re-enacted; it was only ever
renumbered, and it was renumbered because canons *ahead of it* were repealed or
inserted. That is visible on the face of the 1887 and 1889 Digests, where this
canon and "Of the Due Celebration of Sundays" sit adjacent and move together
(20/21 in 1887, 21/22 in 1889).

### Correction to the trail as previously stated

Neither **20 → 22 → 21 → 24** (the brief) nor **20 → 21 → 24** (260835-60's own
summary) is a correct chronological sequence. The "21" reading is real, but it
belongs to one dated Digest, 1886/87, and is bracketed on **both** sides by 22.
The actual sequence is 20 (1874, at once 22) → 22 → 22 → **21** → 22 → 24 → 24
→ repealed.

**Recommendation for deployment.** Do not deploy the numbering. It is a
liability: it looks unstable, it invites a correction that would cost more than
the point is worth, and it is not what the argument needs. Say instead that the
canon kept one title and one text from 1874 to 1904 and changed number only as
its neighbours changed, and cite the 1901 Digest, which shows the canon as
Title I, Canon 24 with the ritual section still in place three years before the
repeal.

### Instrument and year of each renumbering

Established for 1874 only, and there the instrument is the Digest issued with
that Convention's own Journal, which states the renumbering on its face. For
the 1886, 1889 and 1892/1895 shifts, **the instrument was not established**;
what is established is the state at each dated Digest. That renumbering by
express resolution was available as an instrument in this period is shown by
the 1904 Journal, p. 134: "Resolved, the House of Bishops concurring, That
Title I., Canon 13 of the Digest be numbered Canon 20." Whether the earlier
shifts were effected that way or by the compilers was not determined. The
Journals of 1886, 1892 and 1895 were not searched; no Internet Archive item was
located for a General Convention Journal of 1877, 1892, 1895 or 1898.

### Verdict

**§1 — PARTIAL.**

- **Is the canon repealed in 1904 the same canon enacted in 1874? VERIFIED.**
  Continuous title and continuous text at seven dated points, from primary
  Digests, independent of White & Dykman.
- **Is the trail as reported by 260835-60 verified step by step? FALSIFIED as
  a sequence.** The steps exist but not in that order.
- **Instrument and year of each renumbering after 1874: UNMATCHED.**

## §2. The character of the 1904 action — load-bearing

### The question

Was the repeal (a) a targeted removal of this canon on its own merits, or (b)
part of a general revision in which many canons were repealed, renumbered or
consolidated together?

### The answer, stated plainly

**(b).** The 1904 Convention carried out a wholesale recodification of the
canons. A Joint Commission on the Revision of the Canons, chaired by the Bishop
of Albany (Wm. Croswell Doane), continued from the previous Convention,
reported a complete new body of canons. The Convention repealed the Digest and
enacted that new body in its place, to take effect 1 January 1905.

Specifically, from the 1904 Journal:

1. **A general revision was under way, and had been for at least two
   triennia.** Appendix XIV, printed p. 561: "The Joint Commission on the
   Revision of the Canons was continued at the last General Convention. It has
   gone carefully over its last Report both in Committee and in Commission, so
   far as it was not acted upon at the General Convention of 1901, and herewith
   presents the record of its work."

2. **Scale.** The great majority of the Digest, across all four Titles, was
   repealed and re-enacted under new numbers by a series of resolutions in the
   form "Title I., Canon N of the Digest be and is hereby repealed, and that in
   place thereof Canon M, as reported by the Commission, be and is hereby
   enacted." A residual resolution then swept the remainder: "That all the
   Canons of the Digest not heretofore repealed, excepting Title I., Canons 13
   and 21, Title II., Canon 13, Title III., Canons 5 and 7, and Title IV.,
   Canons 1 and 4, be and are hereby repealed." The new body runs to Canon 54,
   whose closing provision is that "These Canons ... shall take effect on the
   first day of January, in the year of our Lord, 1905; from and after which
   day all other Canons of this Church are hereby and shall be deemed to be,
   repealed."

   *An exact count of canons repealed and amended was not established and must
   not be quoted.* The figures above are a demonstrated floor derived from a
   full-text search whose result set may be capped. See the capture file,
   EXCERPT E.

3. **Own resolution, inside a larger schedule.** The repeal of Title I, Canon
   24 is its own resolution, in the standard form. It stands as one item in an
   unbroken schedule of like resolutions carried in a single message — Message
   No. 59 from the House of Deputies, Boston, 14th day of the Session, 21
   October 1904, printed at Journal p. 134. Both descriptions are true and
   neither alone is honest.

4. **But it is one of the exceptions within that schedule.** Most resolutions
   repeal *and re-enact*. The Canon 24 resolution carries no "in place thereof"
   clause. Only three Title I canons and one Title II section were repealed
   bare in the returned matches: Title I Canons 1, 16 and 24, and Title II
   Canon 13 § v. **This is the finding that actually does work.** The
   recodification explains the *occasion* of the repeal; it does not explain
   the *omission*. The Commission simply did not carry the ritual canon
   forward.

5. **Committee report, debate or memorial on this canon in particular: NONE.**
   The Commission's covering report (p. 561) discusses only its membership,
   what the 1901 Convention had already adopted, and three subjects it declined
   to report on (Provinces; the Domestic and Foreign Missionary Society; Courts
   of Appeal). It says nothing about the ritual canon. A full-text search of the
   whole Journal for "Revision of the Canons" returns 28 matches, all
   procedural, naming particular canons by number (11, 12, 13, 14 and 15, 27,
   36, 47) — never Canon 24 of the Digest, never ritual, never ceremonial.

6. **Anything enacted in its place, then or later, addressing ritual or
   ceremonial: NONE found.** No canon in the new body bears the title "Of the
   Use of the Book of Common Prayer" — the phrase returns zero matches in the
   entire 1904 Journal. What the same Convention *did* enact touching worship
   is 1904 **Canon 15, "Of Ministers and Their Duties," § I [i]**: "The control
   of the worship and the spiritual jurisdiction of the Parish, are vested in
   the Rector, subject to the Rubrics of the Book of Common Prayer, the Canons
   of the Church, and the godly counsel of the Bishop." That canon was enacted
   in place of Title I, Canons 18, 19 § i [2] and 20 § iii of the Digest, **not
   in place of Canon 24.** It is not a successor to the ritual canon. It is,
   from 1905, where the control of worship sits.

7. **Incense named anywhere in the 1904 proceedings: NO.** A full-text search
   of the 1904 Journal for "incense" returns no matches. So does "ritual".
   "Ceremonies" returns eight matches, all accounted for: six are the Prayer
   Book's own title-page formula, one is a resolution about the expenses of
   consecrating Missionary Bishops, and one is a passing line in a Bishop's
   address. None concerns the repeal.

   *Caveat, and it must travel with the claim.* These are null results over an
   OCR-derived full-text index of an ~840-leaf volume with uneven OCR. Report
   them as "a full-text search of the 1904 Journal returns no occurrence of
   ...", not as "the word does not occur".

### Corroboration (not load-bearing)

White & Dykman, *Annotated Constitution and Canons ... 1789-1952*, vol. 1
(1954), p. 411, under "CONVENTION OF 1904", says the canon "was repealed by
this Convention, and without a single voice being raised in its defense." That
sentence is consistent with everything above. It is now corroborative rather
than load-bearing: the identification of Title I, Canon 24 as the ritual canon,
which 260835-60 rested on W&D, is established in this pass from the primary
1901 Digest.

### Verdict

**§2 — VERIFIED. The answer is (b), with a material qualification.**

The repeal was an incident of a general recodification of the entire body of
canons, carried out by a standing Joint Commission over two triennia and
enacted as a schedule of resolutions in a single message. It was **not** a
targeted removal of this canon on its own merits, and there is no recorded
deliberation about it of any kind.

The qualification, which is the part worth deploying: within that
recodification the pattern was repeal-and-re-enact, and the ritual canon is one
of a small handful repealed with nothing enacted in its place. The Convention
was not silently dropping a canon it had overlooked; it was rewriting the whole
book and chose not to carry this one into it.

## §3. What the word "silently" can bear

Each formulation is tested on the record established above. The instruction was
not to soften a defeated formulation into a survivable one, so a defeated one is
recorded as defeated.

### (a) "Repealed in 1904."

**SURVIVES.**

Supporting: Journal of the General Convention 1904, p. 134, Message No. 59 from
the House of Deputies, 21 October 1904 — "Resolved, the House of Bishops
concurring, That Title I., Canon 24 of the Digest be and is hereby repealed."
Printed a second time in the House of Deputies' parallel record at leaf 367.
The House of Bishops' concurrence in Message No. 59 is recorded at leaf 394, with
amendments touching Canon 25 § II. only. Identification of Canon 24 as the
ritual canon: the 1901 Digest, primary.

One precision, if pressed: the *act* is 1904; the new canons, and with them the
extinction of the old Digest, took effect **1 January 1905** under Canon 54.
"Repealed in 1904" is right. "Ceased to have effect in 1904" would not be.

### (b) "Repealed, with nothing enacted in its place."

**SURVIVES, but only if "in its place" means what the resolution means.**

Supporting: the Canon 24 resolution carries no "in place thereof" clause, unlike
most of its neighbours in the same schedule; no canon in the new body of 54
bears the title "Of the Use of the Book of Common Prayer"; the phrase returns
zero matches in the whole 1904 Journal; no successor canon on ritual or
ceremonial was enacted in 1904 or has been found since.

Defeating, if the phrase is heard as "the field was left unregulated": the same
Convention enacted Canon 15 § I [i], vesting the control of worship in the
Rector subject to the Rubrics, the Canons, and the godly counsel of the Bishop.
That is not a replacement for the ritual canon — it replaced three other
provisions — but a reader who hears "nothing in its place" as "no canon
governed worship afterwards" has been misled, and the correction will be made.

**Safe form:** "repealed, with no canon enacted in its place, and no canon on
ritual or ceremonial enacted since." That is fully supported and cannot be
turned around.

### (c) "Repealed silently."

**DOES NOT SURVIVE.**

Supporting, and it is not nothing: no debate, no committee report, no memorial
on this canon; the words "ritual" and "incense" do not appear in the Journal at
all; W&D, "without a single voice being raised in its defense"; the Episcopal
Dictionary, "quietly repealed".

Defeating: "silently" carries, and in a public exchange will be heard to carry,
the implication of a stealth or targeted removal — something slipped out while
no one was looking. The record does not support that implication and refutes it
directly. The 1904 Convention repealed almost the entire Digest and enacted 54
new canons reported by a Joint Commission that had been working on them since
before 1901. Nothing about this canon was quieter than anything else in that
schedule; the Journal records no debate on **any** of the repeal resolutions
individually. Deploying "silently" hands the other side a clean and correct
rebuttal — "it was a general recodification" — and once that rebuttal lands, the
real finding in §2(4) goes down with it.

There is also a method problem. General Convention Journals record motions,
messages and votes; they do not record debate. Absence of debate in the Journal
is therefore weaker evidence than it looks, and this pass did not consult
contemporary periodical reporting for October 1904. "Silently" is a claim about
what was said, resting on a source that does not record what was said.

**Do not deploy "silently".** Not as a softened version of itself either.

### (d) "Repealed without recorded discussion of incense."

**SURVIVES. This is the strongest of the four.**

Supporting: full-text search of the 1904 Journal returns no occurrence of
"incense" and none of "ritual"; the eight occurrences of "ceremonies" are all
accounted for and none concerns the repeal; the Joint Commission's covering
report says nothing about the canon; the 28 procedural references to the
revision never name Canon 24. It is also the claim the project actually needs,
since the 1874 canon as enacted did not name incense either — the incense
specifications were struck out in the House of Bishops before enactment.

Carry the OCR caveat with it.

### Verdict

**§3 — (a), (b) and (d) survive; (c) does not.**

Three of four survive. (b) survives only in the tightened form given above.
(c) is defeated and should be dropped rather than reworded.

**Recommended single sentence for deployment**, which says everything supported
and nothing that can be turned around:

> The canon was repealed in 1904, as part of a general revision in which the
> Church rewrote its whole body of canons; most canons were repealed and
> re-enacted under new numbers, but this one was repealed with nothing put in
> its place, and the Journal of that Convention records no discussion of it,
> and does not contain the word "incense" or the word "ritual" anywhere.

## §4. Standing after the repeal

### What governed ritual or ceremonial from 1904 onward

Nothing replaced the 1874 canon's machinery — the bishop's duty to summon the
Standing Committee, investigate, admonish in writing, and on disregard cause the
minister to be tried for breach of his ordination vow. That machinery ended with
the canon.

What remained, on the evidence gathered:

1. **The Constitution.** Article VIII as it stood in 1904 requires that the Book
   of Common Prayer "shall be in use in all the Dioceses and Missionary
   Districts of this Church" and that no alteration or addition be made except
   by the two-Convention process (1904 Journal, leaf 696).

2. **The rubrics of the Book of Common Prayer**, and the Book's own prefatory
   matter, including "Of Ceremonies, why some be abolished and some retained"
   (repo capture `src/SRC_PRIMARY_1662_BCP_Of-Ceremonies...`).

3. **1904 Canon 15, "Of Ministers and Their Duties," § I [i]** — the control of
   worship vested in the Rector, "subject to the Rubrics of the Book of Common
   Prayer, the Canons of the Church, and the godly counsel of the Bishop." This
   provision survives in the current canons as Title III, Canon 9, Sec.
   6(a)(1). This is the instrument that, from 1905 to the present, locates
   discretion over ceremonial — and it locates it in the Rector under the
   Bishop, not in a canon of prohibitions.

4. **The general discipline canons.** Breach of ordination vow remained an
   offence; what disappeared was the specific ritual procedure and the specific
   ground of "ceremonies or practices ... setting forth or symbolizing erroneous
   or doubtful doctrines".

**What was searched.** The 1904 Journal, by full-text search, for "ritual",
"ceremonies", "incense", "Use of the Book of Common Prayer", "Due Celebration of
Sundays", "control of the worship", "Ministers and Their Duties", "Revision of
the Canons", "repealed". The existing repo captures for 1892/1928/1979 prayer
books and for TEC canons since 1874
(`src/SRC_PRIMARY_1892-1979_TEC_PrayerBooks-Canons_Incense-Search.txt`), which
record: no TEC canon naming incense, 1874 to date; two 1979 BCP rubrics naming
incense conditionally, classified there as MENTION WITHOUT GRANT; and the
Rector's control of worship as the operative locus of discretion. **Not
searched:** the Journals of 1907 through 1943 for any later ritual canon; the
current Constitution and Canons in full (the prior pass's retrieval truncated at
Title I, Canon 5, and that null result is recorded there as partial). A later
ritual canon is not asserted to be absent; it is asserted that none was found
and that the search of the intervening Journals was not run.

### Cross-check against 260835-60's EMC finding

260835-60 recorded that Canon 20, Title I is not carried into the Episcopal
Missionary Church baseline, EMC Title I ending at Canon 11, and treated this as
notable.

**It is not notable, and the file should say so.** The 1874 canon was repealed in
1904. The EMC's constitution and canons date from the 1990s
(`src/SRC_PRIMARY_1992-1998_EMC_Constitution-Canons_PrayerBook-and-Ceremonial.txt`).
A canon that had been dead for roughly ninety years before the EMC existed could
not have been "carried into" its baseline, and its absence records no EMC
choice whatever. Presenting it as an EMC omission would impute a decision that
was never made — and it is exactly the kind of imputation the project's
attribution discipline exists to prevent.

What the EMC capture *does* establish independently, and what remains usable, is
the positive finding already recorded there: the EMC's entire Worship title is
three canons (Bible translation, Prayer Book, music), and no EMC canon addresses
ceremonial, ritual or ornaments at all. That finding stands on its own and does
not need the 1874 canon to prop it up.

### Verdict

**§4 — VERIFIED.**

Nothing replaced the ritual canon. From 1905 the governing instruments are the
Constitution's requirement of the Prayer Book's use, the Book's own rubrics, and
1904 Canon 15 § I [i] vesting the control of worship in the Rector subject to
Rubrics, Canons and the Bishop's godly counsel — the provision that survives as
Title III.9.6(a)(1) today. No successor canon on ritual or ceremonial was found;
the intervening Journals were not searched, and that gap is recorded rather than
papered over.

**On the EMC cross-check: the 260835-60 observation is UNREMARKABLE and should
be demoted.** The canon's absence from a 1990s EMC canon set is a consequence of
its repeal in 1904, not evidence about the EMC.
