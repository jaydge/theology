PART 1 — STATUS OF THE 1899 ARCHBISHOPS' OPINION
=====================================================================

--- 1.1 ------------------------------------------------------------
WHAT PROCEDURE THE 1899 OPINION WAS GIVEN UNDER — IN THE OPINION'S
OWN WORDS (its closing paragraph).
SOURCE A. URL: https://www.anglicanhistory.org/liturgy/archbishops_incense1899.html

"In conclusion we think it our duty to press not only on the Clergy
that have appeared before us, but also on all the Clergy alike, to
submit to Episcopal authority in all such matters as these. All alike
have consented to the Book of Common Prayer; and the Book of Common
Prayer requires all persons, not only if they doubt but if they find
that others disagree with them concerning the meaning of the directions
contained in the Book, to resort to the Bishop of the Diocese, who may,
if he think fit, send the question to the Archbishop for his decision.
In order to give the fullest opportunity to any who diversely take any
question of this kind to give reasons for their opinion we have
suspended our decision until we had heard the [15/16] matter fully and
learnedly argued before us; and we have now given our decision as the
Prayer Book requires us to do. We entreat the Clergy, for the sake of
the peace of the Church, which we all so much desire, to accept our
decision thus conscientiously given in the name of our common Master,
the Supreme Head of the Church, the Lord whose commission we bear."

[CAPTURE COMMENT — not source. The Opinion locates its own authority
in the Book of Common Prayer's own provision ("Concerning the Service
of the Church" — resort to the Bishop, who may send the question to the
Archbishop), and it closes by *entreating* acceptance, not by ordering
under any statutory or curial power. Its self-description is a
"decision" given under a Prayer Book rubric, not a judgment of a court.]

--- 1.2 ------------------------------------------------------------
THE OPINION'S OWN STATEMENT THAT INCENSE IS NOT PERMANENTLY EXCLUDED,
AND ITS NAMED ROUTE TO FUTURE AUTHORIZATION.
SOURCE A. Same URL.

"Yet it is right to observe that even now the liturgical use of incense
is not by law permanently excluded from the Church's Ritual. The
Section in Elizabeth's Act which allows the Crown, with the consent of
the Archbishop of Canterbury, to order new ceremonies does not forbid
the inclusion of the use of incense in such new ceremonies if such are
ordered. It would always be possible, if some great occasion made it
suitable, for the Sovereign with the advice of the Primate to order a
great ceremonial in which [12/13] the use of incense should form a
part. The question of probability need not now be raised. Many things
might become probable when our toleration of one another had risen to a
higher level, which are not probable at present. But meanwhile the law
requires that the Clergy should wait for the action of lawful authority
before bringing any additional ceremonial to form a part of public
worship."

And the operative conclusion:

"And we are obliged to come to the conclusion that the use of incense
in the public worship, and as a part of that worship, is not at present
enjoined nor permitted by the law of the Church of England; and it is
our duty to request the Clergy who so use it to discontinue that use.
If used at all, it must be used (in George Herbert's language) to
sweeten the Church, and outside the worship altogether."

[CAPTURE COMMENT — not source. This matters for the research question:
the 1899 Opinion itself names the ONE mechanism it regarded as capable
of positively authorizing incense — an order of the Crown with the
consent of the Archbishop of Canterbury under the section of the Act of
Uniformity 1559. No pass has found any such order. And the section
relied on was itself swept away in 1974 (see 1.4).]

--- 1.3 ------------------------------------------------------------
CONTEMPORARY PARLIAMENTARY CHARACTERISATION OF THE LAMBETH HEARING AS
NOT A COURT. Earl of Portsmouth, House of Lords, 16 July 1900.
SOURCE B. URL: https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/lords/1900/jul/16/ritualism-in-the-church

"...ot call upon me to obey this opinion, therefore I am not bound by
it." Now, my Lords, what does that mean? It means that, instead of the
opinions of the two Archbishops being taken and recognised as binding
morally upon the clergy, the words are practically only a phrase and
simply carry with them moral authority where and when a particular
Bishop in a particular diocese chooses to enforce it. I venture to say
that that entirely and absolutely gives away the whole case and the
whole reason for this tribunal; it is not a legal tribunal, but it was
supposed to..."

[TRANSCRIPTION NOTE: the excerpt begins and ends mid-sentence because
it was extracted by regex window from the Hansard page's single long
text block; the leading "...ot call upon me" is the tail of "my bishop
does not call upon me to obey this opinion". The load-bearing clause,
complete, is: "it is not a legal tribunal".]

Opening of the same speech:

"My Lords, in rising to call attention to the continued lawlessness in
the Church of England, and to ask the Prime Minister if he is prepared
to give effect to the Resolution of the House of Commons of Wednesday,
10th May, 1899, I shall confine my remarks to those particular cases in
which the clergy have not only revolted against the lawful authority of
the highest Court, but against the opinions of the two Archbishops and
the moral authority of their ecclesiastical superiors."

CLASSIFICATION of 1.1-1.3: not an authorization question. These
establish that the 1899 Opinion was an archiepiscopal Opinion under a
Prayer Book reference procedure, treated by its critics in Parliament
within a year as carrying moral, not curial, authority.

THE STATUTORY REGIME THE 1899 OPINION RESTED ON, AND ITS REPEAL.

(a) SCHEDULE 2 — Acts and Measures Repealed (extract of the rows that

[CAPTURE COMMENT — not source. "The Act of Uniformity 1558" (1 Eliz. 1

prohibition it quotes at length, and the Crown-with-the-Archbishop

strongest sourced statement available that the 1899 Opinion's legal
foundation no longer exists. It is NOT a reversal of the Opinion by
name — see 1.6.]

(b) SECTION 1 — Provision by Canon for worship (extract).
URL: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukcm/1974/3/section/1

"(1) It shall be lawful for the General Synod—
 (a) to make provision by Canon with respect to worship in the Church
 of England, including provision for empowering the General Synod to
 approve, amend, continue or discontinue forms of service;

 shall have effect notwithstanding anything inconsistent therewith
 contained in any of the rubrics in the Book of Common Prayer."

"(5) Without prejudice to the generality of subsection (1) of this
 section, the General Synod may make provision by Canon—
 (a) for empowering the Convocations, the archbishops and the bishops

 no provision is made by forms of service contained in the Book of
 Common Prayer or approved by the General Synod or the Convocations
 under Canon;
 (b) for empowering any minister to make and use minor variations in
 the forms of service contained in the said Book or approved by the

(c) SECTION 5 — Interpretation (extract).
URL: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukcm/1974/3/section/5/data.xht?view=snippet&wrap=true

"'form of service' means any order, service, prayer, rite or ceremony
whatsoever, including the services for the ordination of priests and
deacons and the consecration of bishops and the catechism or form of
instruction before confirmation;"

"'rubrics' of the Book of Common Prayer include all directions and
instructions contained in the said Book, and all tables, prefaces,
rules, calendars and other contents thereof."

CLASSIFICATION: the 1974 Measure is not a POSITIVE AUTHORIZATION of
incense — it does not name incense at any point. It is the instrument
that removed the statutory regime under which incense was held
unlawful, and vested the discretion elsewhere (General Synod by Canon;
minister and bishop under the canons made pursuant to it). On the
schema supplied, its effect on incense is best described as
PERMISSION BY SILENCE.

--- 1.5 ------------------------------------------------------------
THE MODERN COMMENTARY IS DIVIDED. DO NOT REPORT THIS AS SETTLED.

(a) Philip Jones, arguing that the Victorian ceremonial case law is no
longer law at all. SOURCE D.
URL: https://ecclesiasticallaw.wordpress.com/2019/04/26/smells-and-bells-services-and-ceremonies/

"The Act of Uniformity was *repealed* by the Worship and Doctrine
Measure 1974."

"Yet, subject to the narrow exceptions just mentioned, the 1662 regime
of public worship *is no more. It has ceased to be.* The voluminous
case law concerning liturgical ceremonies can therefore no longer be
good law."

"The repeal of the 1662 regime could not, of course, have the effect of
reviving or reinstating the mediaeval liturgical practices abolished at
the Reformation. However, it *does* mean that liturgical practices not
retained or referred to in the Book of Common Prayer are *prima facie*
permitted, no longer forbidden – subject, of course, to the canons made
by the General Synod under s.1 of the Worship and Doctrine Measure."

But Jones expressly records that the point is contested, and that the
leading commentator takes the opposite view:

"Chancellor Bursell considered that the reference to 'forms of
services' in the liturgical canons does indeed include ceremonies, and
that therefore ceremonies are regulated by canons B1 to B5 (cf *St
John's, Chopwell* (1995) 3 Weekly Law Reports 606, p.611 and p.615)."

"If Bursell is right about this (and he may be) then *prima facie* the
position will be similar to that which obtained in Victorian times.
Ceremonies will be just as illegal now as they were then, unless they
are clearly permitted by the rubrics of an official service."

And on the possibility that the canons deliberately say nothing:

"Thus the explicit reference to ceremonies in the 1974 Measure does not
appear in the liturgical canons, either in canon B1(3) or elsewhere."
"It is hard to believe that this omission was accidental. There may
have been a deliberate policy of silence on the subject, to avoid
reviving old disputes..."

(b) Law & Religion UK, describing the position as settled in practice
while citing Bursell's reliance on the 1899 Opinion itself.
SOURCE E. URL: https://lawandreligionuk.com/2015/07/31/incense-and-the-psychoactive-substances-bill/

"At least ecclesiastical law in this area is now settled, following a
period during which its ceremonial use was held to be illegal,"

"However, Bursell states that in the light of the Archbishops' comments
in *The Lawfulness of the Liturgical Use of Incense and the Carrying of
Lights in Procession*, 'it can hardly be suggested that the ceremonial
use of incense is doctrinally unacceptable'. Nevertheless, a faculty is
still required for the introduction of the necessary hardware."
   [footnote 3: R D H Bursell, *Liturgy, Order and the Law*,
    (Clarendon Press, 1996) 77. footnote 4: Lambeth Palace, July 31, 1899.]

(c) Law & Religion UK on how far ceremonial outside Canon B 5 is
lawful at all. SOURCE F.
URL: https://lawandreligionuk.com/2016/04/05/monstrances-in-the-church-of-england-are-they-legal/

"Briden summarizes the current legal position [page 136] like this:
'any modification in public worship outside the scope of Canon B 5 is
introduced at the minister's peril. The peril is not great, as
proceedings are today unlikely to be taken against him or her; but in
the last century it was real'."

[CAPTURE COMMENT — not source. Note the irony worth preserving: the
argument that ceremonial incense is now doctrinally unobjectionable is
made by Bursell *out of the 1899 Opinion's own reasoning* (the Opinion
said the Church "has never spoken of incense as an evil thing" and that
it was excluded "not as an evil thing, but as unsuited to the needs of
the day"). That is a use of the Opinion, not a reversal of it.]

--- 1.6 ------------------------------------------------------------
UNMATCHED — NO INSTRUMENT FORMALLY REVERSING THE 1899 OPINION WAS FOUND.

Plainly stated: this pass found NO Measure, NO Canon, NO judgment of
any court (Arches, Chancery Court of York, Court of Ecclesiastical
Causes Reserved, Judicial Committee), NO synodical act, and NO later
archiepiscopal opinion that reverses, rescinds, revokes, overrules or
sets aside the Archbishops' Opinion of 31 July 1899 on incense, or that
grants liturgical incense by name.

What was found instead is (i) the wholesale repeal in 1974 of the Acts
of Uniformity 1548, 1558 and (all but ss.10, 15) 1662, i.e. of the
statutory regime the Opinion construed, and (ii) an unresolved
scholarly dispute (1.5) about whether that repeal carried the Victorian
ceremonial case law with it.

*** THE ONE PERMISSIVE RUBRIC NAMING INCENSE THAT THIS PASS FOUND ***
COMMON WORSHIP: DAILY PRAYER — "The Blessing of the Light", in Morning
and Evening Prayer. Read directly by the capturing agent.
SOURCE H. URL: https://daily.commonworship.com/morneve/light.html
Mirrors carrying the identical wording:
  https://justus.anglican.org/~ss/commonworship/daily/morneve/light.html
  https://cwdp.oremus.org/morneve/light.html

Context, verbatim, in sequence (rubrics in the source are set in
italic; the "All" prefixes mark congregational text):

"## ¶ The Blessing of the Light

The Blessing of Light may replace the Preparation at Evening Prayer on
any occasion.

A lamp or candle may be lit.
   ...
Other candles may be lit as Phos Hilaron – A Song of the Light (page
635), the following, or another suitable hymn or canticle, is sung
   [Hail, gladdening Light ... tr. John Keble]

Verses from Psalm 141 – A Song of the Evening Sacrifice
 may be said and incense may be burned

 All  Let my prayer rise before you as incense, •
 the lifting up of my hands as the evening sacrifice.
   ...
 Psalm 141.1-4b, 8"

THE OPERATIVE WORDS, ISOLATED:
   "may be said and incense may be burned"

CLASSIFICATION: **POSITIVE AUTHORIZATION, by name, permissive, but with
a decisive qualification about the status of the book it sits in — see
the caveat immediately below.** It is a rubric in the body of a service
book, it names incense, and it permits the act ("incense may be
burned"). Conditions attached in the rubric itself: none — no bishop's
direction, no limit of manner, no restriction of the thurifer. It is
confined by position: it authorizes burning incense at one point of one
optional opening rite (the Blessing of the Light replacing the
Preparation at Evening Prayer), during Psalm 141.

*** STATUS CAVEAT, WHICH MUST TRAVEL WITH THIS FINDING ***
Common Worship: Daily Prayer is understood to be COMMENDED by the House
of Bishops as material suitable for use under the minister's Canon B 5
discretion, NOT AUTHORIZED by the General Synod. If that is right, the
rubric is not an act of the Church's legislature granting incense; it
is the House of Bishops commending a form in which incense is named,
and the actual permission still runs through Canon B 5 and the bishop.
This pass did NOT obtain a primary-source statement of that status —
see the UNMATCHED entry at 4(c). Do not describe this rubric as a
General Synod authorization until that is verified.

CAPTURE FOOTER — added by pass 260835-60, not part of the sources.

WHAT THIS ESTABLISHES (and what it does not):

 1. NOTHING FOUND REVERSED THE 1899 OPINION BY ANY INSTRUMENT. No
    Measure, Canon, judgment, synodical act or later archiepiscopal
    opinion naming and setting it aside was located. What happened
    instead is structural: the Church of England (Worship and
    Doctrine) Measure 1974 repealed the Act of Uniformity 1558 (the
    Opinion's whole statutory basis) and the Act of Uniformity 1662
    except ss. 10 and 15, and vested worship regulation in the General
    Synod by Canon.

 2. THE 1899 OPINION WAS NOT A COURT JUDGMENT, ON ITS OWN ACCOUNT.
    It was given under the Prayer Book's own "resort to the Bishop,
    who may send the question to the Archbishop" provision, and it
    ends by entreating acceptance. Within a year a peer told the Lords
    of the Lambeth hearing that "it is not a legal tribunal". This
    dovetails exactly with the 1906 Royal Commission's statement,
    already captured by pass 260835-56, that the incense question
    "has never been before the Judicial Committee".

 3. THE 1928 DEPOSITED BOOK DOES NOT NAME INCENSE IN ANY RUBRIC, NOTE,
    TABLE, APPENDIX OR PREFACE. The single occurrence of the word in
    the whole transcribed book is Malachi 1:11 as an Epiphany opening
    Sentence. And the book disclaims its own authorization in its own
    front matter: "The publication of this Book does not directly or
    indirectly imply that it can be regarded as authorized for use in
    churches." Both Commons resolutions were defeated — 15 December
    1927 and 14 June 1928 — so the book was never presented for the
    Royal Assent.

 4. THE ASB 1980, so far as retrievable, DOES NOT NAME INCENSE — not
    in the General Notes, not in the 24 Notes to Rite A, not in the
    Calendar and Rules.

 5. THE STRONGEST POST-1900 TEXT FOUND THAT NAMES INCENSE
    PERMISSIVELY IS A SINGLE RUBRIC IN COMMON WORSHIP: DAILY PRAYER,
    at the Blessing of the Light: "Verses from Psalm 141 – A Song of
    the Evening Sacrifice may be said and incense may be burned."
    Its force depends entirely on whether that volume is AUTHORIZED or
    merely COMMENDED — an open question on this pass (4(c)). Every
    other Common Worship occurrence is either an incense *object*
    (Easter Candle studs), an incense *gift* (Epiphany), commentary
    or planning material, or Scripture.

 6. DO NOT REPORT THE PRESENT LAW AS SETTLED. Philip Jones holds the
    Victorian ceremonial case law "can therefore no longer be good
    law"; Bursell holds that ceremonies ARE regulated by canons B1-B5,
    on which reading "Ceremonies will be just as illegal now as they
    were then, unless they are clearly permitted by the rubrics of an
    official service". Jones records that Bursell "may be" right. Both
    readings must be kept live.
