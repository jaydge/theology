ON INCENSE — every passage the article contains
=====================================================================

p. 581, the 1871 enactment:
  "Canon 37 banned the elevation of the host during communion, 38 incense, and
  39 processions."

p. 584, the Irish Church Union's complaints in the early twentieth century:
  "For over forty years, it was a thorn in the side of any (even slightly)
  ritualistic Irish churchman, regularly complaining about unlawful vestments,
  uncanonical gestures, the use of candles and incense, genuflection, and
  failure to stand at the north side of the altar."

⭐ p. 591, the 1974 revision:
  "Other prohibitions, however, on the use of incense, candles on the altar,
  and the elevation of the host, were retained."

⭐ p. 591, the position by the end of the twentieth century:
  "And by the end of the twentieth century, even practices that were still
  prohibited, such as the use of incense, were silently winked at. Churches
  such as St. George's in Belfast, and St. Bartholomew's in Dublin could, as a
  result, worship in a way that would have been unthinkable in the decades
  after 1871."

⚠️ THE ARTICLE NOWHERE STATES THAT THE INCENSE BAN WAS REPEALED OR AMENDED —
not in 1964, not in 1974, not in 1984, not later. Its last word on incense is
that it stood and was ignored. This is consistent with the primary finding that
the canon's text is unchanged.

ADOPTION, DATES, AND THE ANTI-RITUALIST CONTEXT
=====================================================================

p. 576: "The British Parliament ... in 1869 cut the Church of Ireland loose
from the state" ... "Since disestablishment was to come into effect on January
1, 1871, this posed some urgent questions."

p. 577: "the representative Convention of bishops, clergy, and laity, which met
between April and November 1870, and in the Church's first General Synod, which
convened on April 13, 1871."

p. 580: "In April 1870 the Convention appointed a Judicature Committee to revise
the old Irish canons of 1634."

⭐ p. 580, the point of the exercise:
  "Disestablishment meant that the Church of Ireland was now free to draw up
  its own canons, having since the Act of Union been bound by those of the
  Church of England. Irish churchmen and women were concerned at the vagueness
  of the existing ornaments rubric, and the way in which ritualist clergy in
  the Church of England had flouted it. They thus drew up a new code that
  defined precisely (and, they hoped, inescapably) church ornamentation and
  clerical dress and behavior in a way that ruled out high-church practices."

⭐ p. 581, the whole ritual-canon cluster in one sentence:
  "New 'ritual canons' were drawn up by the Committee and debated by Synod
  between April and May 1871. Canon 4 defined what clergy were to wear: 'a
  plain white surplice' with 'the customary scarf of plain black silk.' At
  communion, Canon 5 required that the minister to say the prayer of
  consecration while standing at the north of the table—this was directed
  against ritualists who prayed facing the altar with their backs to the
  congregation. It also forbade the minister bowing to the table or making the
  sign of the cross during service. Canon 35 prohibited lit candles during
  service, unless needed for light. Canon 37 banned the elevation of the host
  during communion, 38 incense, and 39 processions. Canon 40 made changes to
  church ornaments dependent on the consent of incumbent, select vestry, and
  bishop, with provision for aggrieved parties to appeal to the diocesan
  court."

p. 581, on Canon 36 (the cross):
  "Canon 36 certainly marked an unusual, indeed unprecedented step for a church
  in the Anglican communion: banning a central symbol of Christianity."
  "This had not appeared in the draft prepared for the Synod but had been
  proposed late in the day by a Clogher clergyman, George Finlay."

pp. 579-580, the mood:
  "A 'Memorial,' signed by over four thousand clergymen and vestrymen was
  presented to the Convention, calling on it 'to prevent any departure from the
  pure simplicity of our worship, and to guard against the introduction of
  superstitious novelties tending to assimilate any of our services to those of
  the Church of Rome.'"

p. 578, the voting mechanism that made the canons hard to change:
  "But the threat of lay dictatorship was offset by a further provision that
  voting on important issues could be by order, and that for a measure to pass
  it had to have two-thirds majority support among the clergy and the laity."

⚠️ DISCREPANCY WITH PRIMARY RECORD, FLAGGED NOT RESOLVED. Ford dates the ritual
canons throughout to April-May 1871 and the delegated reader reports that the
article "contains NO reference to 1877 at all." The 1878 Prayer Book volume's
own adoption line reads "at General Synods held in Dublin in the years of our
Lord 1871 and 1877", and the volume prints, after Canon 48, a separate group
headed "ADDITIONAL CANONS, 1877" (numbered I-VI, on repelling notorious sinners
from Holy Communion). ⭐ THE MOST LIKELY RECONCILIATION — and it is a
reconciliation, not a verified finding — is that the ritual canons proper
(1-48, including Canon 38 on incense) are of 1871, and 1877 supplied only the
appended Additional Canons, the composite volume therefore carrying both dates
on its heading. This has NOT been confirmed against the General Synod journals.
⛔ Until it is, cite Canon 38 as "the Constitutions and Canons Ecclesiastical
of the Church of Ireland, agreed by the General Synods of 1871 and 1877 as
printed in the 1878 Prayer Book volume" — which is exactly what the volume
itself says and is true on either reconciliation.

Ford's own citation for the canon text (n. 37, p. 581):
  "The Statutes Passed in the General Synod of the Church of Ireland 1871
  (Dublin: Edward Purdon, 1874), 8-9, 18-19: Bray, The Anglican Canons, lxviii,
  838-856."
  [⭐ These are the primary sources to obtain if the 1871/1877 question needs
  settling.]

LATER AMENDMENT — the dates, and what did NOT change
=====================================================================

FAILED attempts on Canon 36 (the cross):
  1890s (p. 583): "Efforts were made by the PDA at General Synod to change the
    wording of Canon 36 ... but they ran into the inevitable roadblock of the
    voting system."
  World War I (p. 585): "Warre Wells ... led a campaign to repeal 'that insult
    to Christendom, commonly called the 36th Canon.'" ... "The campaign got
    nowhere."
  1930 (p. 585): "A concerted effort by Bishops Day of Ossory and Orr of Meath
    to repeal Canon 36 reached General Synod in 1930. They secured majority
    support from the clergy—83 to 32 ... but lost overwhelmingly among the
    laity—32 in favor, 136 against."

⭐ 1964 — the cross (pp. 588-589, and abstract p. 575):
  "Finally, in 1964 a motion to revise Canon 36 came before the General Synod."
  "two laymen, one from the north, the other from the south, proposed a motion
  to allow the placing of a cross on the communion table or behind it,
  providing that the incumbent and the select vestry obtained the permission of
  the bishop."
  "It was passed by the clergy by 153 votes to 10, and by the laity by 120
  votes to 53."
  Abstract, p. 575: "It was only in 1964 that the ban on the cross was
  removed".
  p. 589, argument used: "The Church of Ireland was alone among the twenty
  churches in the Anglican communion in banning it."

⭐ 1971-1974 — the general revision (pp. 590-591):
  "In 1971 the Church finally took appointed a committee to revise the canons.
  Meeting repeatedly from 1971 to 1974, it drew up a new set of canons, finally
  approved by General Synod in 1974."
  [The words "finally took appointed" appear so in the extraction; flagged, not
  corrected.]
  "The committee was chaired for its first two years by the new Bishop of
  Clogher, Richard Hanson." ... his principle: the church should be governed
  "by freedom of the gospel and not by yoke of law."
  ⭐ "Some provisions of the 1871 canons were removed, such as the ban on the
  sign of the cross at communion and on bowing to the table. Processions were
  allowed at the beginning and end of service. Other prohibitions, however, on
  the use of incense, candles on the altar, and the elevation of the host, were
  retained. But the most contentious issue was that hoary chestnut, dating
  right back to the reign of Edward VI, of clerical vestments."
  "The final version stated that clergy might wear a cassock, and should wear a
  white surplice and a black scarf or colored stole."
  "There was a willingness to remove many of the prohibitions, but that was
  tempered by the desire, particularly strong in the north of Ireland, to
  preserve the 'Protestant' character of the Church of Ireland."
  "The revision of the canons in 1974 represented a major shift in the church's
  approach to regulating liturgical practice. It gave up trying to control
  precisely what clergy did during services and allowed greater freedom."

⭐ 1984 — candles (p. 591):
  "In May 1984 the ban on candles on the altar was removed by General Synod."

⚠️ The article says NOTHING about wafer bread, the mixed chalice, or the
eastward position as distinct canonical items; gives no year for any change to
the elevation prohibition after 1974; gives no year for any change to the
north-side rule; and narrates no canonical change after 1984.

ENFORCEMENT
=====================================================================

p. 582, the courts: "the Convention had established diocesan courts, with
appeals to the Court of the General Synod." ... "the General Synod court had
three episcopal and four lay members."
p. 582: "as was all too evident in England, legal prohibition merely invited
ingenious circumvention."
p. 582, 1872: "an abortive attempt to prosecute William Maturin, perpetual
curate of All Saints' Grangegorman in 1872".
p. 583, 1892, Travers Smith, the leading Canon 36 case: "Smith was found guilty
of having a cross behind the table, contrary to Canon 36, and ordered to remove
it." ... "Smith then placed the cross before the communion table (exactly the
kind of high-church slipperiness that antiritualists feared), and there it
remained."
p. 583, 1928: conviction of Walter Simpson in General Synod Court.
pp. 583-584, Samuel Colquhoun, four appearances 1935, 1937, 1939, 1940: "In
1937 he was convicted by the General Synod Court of bowing to the communion
table, making the sign of the cross during service, and setting up stations of
the cross in the church. His punishment—suspension for six months and payment
of substantial costs—was a clear signal that episcopal tolerance had reached
its limit."
p. 588, the end of enforcement: "The last appeal concerning a ritual canons
case was heard by the Court of the General Synod in 1941, when a charge against
the dean of St. Patrick's was dismissed with costs."
n. 60, p. 584, on evasion: a server at St. John's Sandymount 1940-48 "recalled
that one of his duties before each Sunday service, was to check whether there
were any 'strangers' in the congregation: if there were, the candles on the
altar remained unlit, and the minister refrained from practices that breached
the canons."

⚠️ NO PROSECUTION FOR INCENSE IS NAMED ANYWHERE IN THE ARTICLE. The named
convictions concern the cross, bowing, and the sign of the cross.

RELATION TO THE CHURCH OF ENGLAND; STATUS OF THE CANONS
=====================================================================

p. 576: "As an established church, ultimate authority in the nineteenth-century
Church of Ireland rested with the supreme governor, the English monarch, and
with the English parliament."
p. 580: "having since the Act of Union been bound by those of the Church of
England."
p. 579: "following disestablishment there was considerable resentment at what
was seen as the 'betrayal' of its sister church by the Church of England, and
this fueled hostility to the Church of England, which was seen as 'polluting'
the Church of Ireland by exporting ritualism."
p. 585: "ritualism remained a live and bitterly divisive issue in the Church of
Ireland when it had long since faded into the background in the Church of
England."
⭐ p. 575 (abstract): "The empowerment of the laity left it with a distinctive,
indeed unique, feature among the churches of the Anglican communion—a set of
disciplinary canons designed to exclude high-church ritualism from its
worship."
⭐ p. 591: "The 1871 ritual canons were a unique experiment in the Anglican
communion, seeking to use ecclesiastical democracy to regulate liturgical
practice and church ornamentation."
p. 592: "The ritual canons, according to Anthony Hanson, the Irish-born
professor of theology in the University of Hull, marked the Church of Ireland
as 'the laughing-stock of the Anglican communion.'"

⚠️ The article does NOT discuss the civil-law status of the canons after

⭐ NOTE THE DOUBLE EDGE IN THE WORD "UNIQUE". Ford's judgement that the ritual
canons were unprecedented and unique in the Anglican Communion cuts BOTH ways
for anyone deploying Canon 38/40: it establishes that an Anglican church did
prohibit incense outright, and in the same breath establishes that no other
Anglican church did.
