## 2. The register question — ⭐⭐⭐ **ANSWERED, AND IN JD'S FAVOUR**

The brief raised this against a specific counter-argument RJ may make: that ante-Nicene abstention was **purely circumstantial** — the imperial cult made incense look like emperor-worship — and therefore says nothing about incense in principle.

⭐⭐⭐ **THAT COUNTER-ARGUMENT IS NOT SUPPORTED BY THESE EIGHT TEXTS, AND THE MARGIN IS WIDE.** Six of the eight give an explicitly **theological** reason, stated as a cause, in the author's own voice:

- **Justin, Athenagoras and Lactantius** all argue from **divine non-need** — God does not require, is not fed by, and takes no pleasure in material offerings, because of what He *is*.
- **Barnabas** argues from **divine non-need plus supersession** — the old cult is abolished so that the new law may have its own oblation.
- **Tertullian (*Apology* 30)** argues from **God's own requirement** — the sacrifice God demands is prayer from a chaste body, not purchased incense.
- **Justin (*2 Apology* 5)** argues from the **demonic origin** of the practice.

⛔ **Exactly one of the eight is prudential — `#1`, Tertullian *De Corona* 11 — and that is the one the source table cites at the wrong chapter.** Its reason is idolatry-avoidance: the soldier was not permitted to burn incense **to an idol**. That is a statement about idols, not about incense.

⚠️ **ONE HONEST QUALIFICATION ON THIS RESULT, BECAUSE IT NARROWS WHAT THE SIX ESTABLISH.** Athenagoras's chapter is framed as a **rebuttal to the charge of atheism** and Tertullian's as a **rebuttal to the charge of disloyalty**. The apologetic setting is prudential; **the reason given inside it is not.** The distinction matters and is worth holding: these Fathers were explaining to hostile emperors *why the objection fails*, not confessing to a congregation why the practice is forbidden. ⛔ **That does not convert their stated reasons into prudential ones — but it does mean none of the six is a liturgical directive, and none was written to a church about its own worship.**

## 3. ⛔⛔⛔ **THE FINDING THAT GOVERNS DEPLOYMENT, AND THE BRIEF DID NOT ANTICIPATE IT**

**Winning the register argument does not win the argument.** There is a second gap, and it is larger.

⛔⛔⛔ **EVERY ONE OF THE SIX THEOLOGICAL ARGUMENTS IS ABOUT INCENSE AS A SACRIFICIAL OBLATION OFFERED *TO* GOD, AND EVERY ONE IS MADE WHILE REFUSING PAGAN OR JEWISH SACRIFICIAL CULT. NOT ONE OF THE EIGHT ADDRESSES INCENSE USED CEREMONIALLY OR HONORIFICALLY IN A CHRISTIAN ASSEMBLY.**

The contrast class in each case is a **victim on an altar**:

- Justin's list is *blood, libations, incense* — the furniture of pagan sacrifice.
- Athenagoras's is *blood, the odour of burnt-offerings, the fragrance of flowers and incense* — and his question is *"what need has He further of a hecatomb?"*
- Barnabas's is Isaiah's catalogue of **Jewish** cult — burnt-offerings, fat of lambs, blood of bulls, fine flour, new moons and sabbaths.
- Lactantius's is *gold, silver, purple, silk, a victim, and what is burnt upon the altar.*
- Tertullian's (*Apology* 30) is *incense, wine, and the blood of a worthless ox.*

⛔⛔ **THIS IS THE ANSWER A RITUALIST WILL GIVE, AND IT IS AVAILABLE TO HIM FROM THESE VERY TEXTS: no Anglican defender of the thurible claims incense is a propitiatory sacrifice or that God is fed by it. If that is what the Fathers deny, the denial does not reach the practice.** The distinction is exactly the one `Protestant_Commentary_Survey_Malachi_1_11.md` §1 draws between **B-explicit** and **B-silent**: these eight are, on the ceremonial question, **silent** — and the survey's own governing rule applies here without modification. ⛔ **Counting their silence about censers as a denial of censers is the same error as reading Rev. James's silence as agreement.**

⭐⭐ **AND TWO OF THE EIGHT CARRY POSITIVE COUNTER-EVIDENCE, WHICH IS WORSE THAN SILENCE.**

- **`#1`, *De Corona* 10:** Tertullian says that **he burns frankincense himself** when a place smells bad, and that the substance is a creature of God, innocent in itself — what differs is the ceremony, dress and pomp. **This is in the very chapter the source table cites.**
- **`#3`, *Apology* 42:** Tertullian asserts that Christians consume Arabian and Sabaean incense **as largely in burying their dead as pagans do in fumigating their gods**. Christians were buying incense in quantity.

⛔⛔⛔ **SO THE STRONGEST DEFENSIBLE FORM OF THE CLAIM IS NARROWER THAN THE ONE THE SOURCE TABLE SUPPORTS. These texts establish that the ante-Nicene Fathers rejected incense as a SACRIFICIAL OFFERING and reasoned theologically in doing so. They do NOT establish that the Fathers spoke against incense as such, and two of them show Christians using it.**

⏳ **WHAT WOULD ACTUALLY CLOSE THIS, AND IT IS NOT DONE HERE:** an ante-Nicene text addressing incense **in Christian assembly** and refusing it. None of the eight is one. Whether such a text exists is outside this pass's brief and is **flagged, not answered.** ⚠️ **Note that `LS-25` already records Rev. James conceding that the early patristics run *"continually"* against incense — so the historical direction is not itself in dispute between the parties; what is in dispute is what follows from it.**

## 4. The eight, one by one

### 1. Tertullian, *De Corona* ch. 10 — **(C) WRONG CHAPTER, CORRECT ATTRIBUTION**

**Claim as submitted:** *Soldiers burn incense; Christians do not — and Tertullian explains why.*

⛔⛔ **Chapter 10 is not about soldiers and not about incense.** It is about **crowns and garlands** as idol-dress: the chaplet belongs to idols by its origin and its cultic use, the dead are crowned and thereby become idols, and the chapter closes on Paul's *"Flee idolatry."* Incense enters once, obliquely, as *"the Arabian product,"* and it enters **on the permissive side**: Tertullian's point is that the substances are innocent creatures of God and that what defiles is the rite, not the material. He offers himself as the example — he burns it, only not with the ceremony and pomp given to idols.

**Quotation (12 words):** *"if the smell of some place or other offends me, I burn"* — the sentence continues *the Arabian product myself.*

⭐ **The soldier-and-incense line is real, and it is in CHAPTER 11.** There Tertullian asks whether warfare is proper for Christians at all, and the incense clause is a **subordinate given** inside that argument, not its subject: the Christian *"was not permitted to burn incense to an idol."*

**Register: PRUDENTIAL** (at ch. 11), and narrowly so. The prohibition attaches to the **idol**, not to the incense. At ch. 10 the reason for the crown prohibition is likewise cultic association.

⚠️⚠️ **THIS IS THE PASS'S SHARPEST WARNING. Cited at ch. 10, this reference does not merely fail to support the claim — it supplies the ritualist with Tertullian burning incense in his own house.** ⭐ **ANF's own editor, Coxe, notes at this passage that Tertullian *"seems to know no use for incense except for burials and for fumigation"* — a nineteenth-century Anglican editor reading the text in the direction that helps JD on liturgical use, while confirming domestic use. That is editorial opinion, not primary text, and is recorded as such.**

---

### 2. Tertullian, *Apology* ch. 30 — **(B) CONFIRMED, BUT MISFRAMED**

**Claim as submitted:** *Christians offer no incense to emperors.*

⛔ **The object is wrong, and correcting it makes the citation stronger, not weaker.** Tertullian is not describing what Christians withhold from the emperor. He is describing what he **offers to God on the emperor's behalf**: ceaseless prayer for the emperor's life, the empire's security, the armies, the senate. The incense contrast is between **what God requires** and the cheap material offerings of pagan sacrifice — grains of incense a farthing buys, a few drops of wine, the blood of a worthless ox.

**Quotation (11 words):** *"not the few grains of incense a farthing buys — tears"* — continuing *of an Arabian tree.*

**Register: THEOLOGICAL.** The reason is stated as God's own demand — the sacrifice offered *"at His own requirement"* is prayer from a chaste body, an unstained soul, a sanctified spirit. ⚠️ **But see §3: the contrast class is pagan sacrificial incense, so what is displaced is incense-as-oblation, not incense-as-ceremony.**

⭐ **Coxe's editorial footnote here calls this *"this reflection on the use of material incense, which is common to early Christians"* — recorded as the ANF editor's own generalisation, and again as editorial opinion rather than Tertullian's text.**

---

### 3. Tertullian, *Apology* ch. 42 — **(A) CONFIRMED AS CLAIMED**

**Claim as submitted:** *Christians spend on burial spices, not fumigating gods.*

✅ **Confirmed exactly, and the claim is well drafted — it says what the text says.** The chapter answers the charge that Christians are economically useless: they use the forum, the baths, the markets, they pay their taxes. On incense Tertullian concedes the temple revenues are falling, then turns it: the Sabaeans lose nothing, because Christians spend as much on incense burying their dead as pagans spend fumigating their gods.

**Quotation (5 words):** *"We certainly buy no frankincense."*

**Register: DESCRIPTIVE, with no theological reason given at all.** The argument is commercial. To the extent a reason is implied it is idolatry-avoidance, and it is implied rather than stated.

⛔⛔ **AND THIS IS THE CITATION THAT MOST CLEARLY CUTS AGAINST THE ARGUMENT IT IS DEPLOYED FOR.** *"We certainly buy no frankincense"* is qualified **in the next sentence** by an assertion that Christians buy it in bulk. What Christians decline is the **cultic purchase** — incense for the temples. ⛔ **Deploying the first sentence without the second would be a real misrepresentation, and an opponent who opens the chapter will find it immediately.**

---

### 4. Justin Martyr, *1 Apology* ch. 13 — **(A) CONFIRMED AS CLAIMED**

**Claim as submitted:** *Spiritual worship only; no incense.*

✅ **Confirmed, cleanly, and this is one of the two best of the eight.** Justin opens by denying the atheism charge: Christians worship the Maker of the universe, who needs no blood, no libations, no incense. The positive half is stated as fully as the negative — the honour worthy of God is **not** to consume by fire what He made for our sustenance, but to use it for ourselves and the needy, and to render thanks by invocations and hymns.

**Quotation (11 words):** *"He has no need of streams of blood and libations and incense"*

**Register: THEOLOGICAL**, on two distinct grounds — divine non-need, and a positive doctrine that burning God's provision is the wrong kind of honour. ⭐ **That second ground is the most transferable argument in the whole set: it is not merely "God does not need it" but "consuming it by fire is not what it is for."**

⭐ **Corroboration from the same work, not in the source table:** at **ch. 37** Justin quotes Isaiah's *"if you bring fine flour, incense, it is abomination unto Me"* — the same oracle Barnabas uses at `#6`. Recorded as a cross-reference, not as a ninth citation.

### 5. Justin Martyr, *2 Apology* ch. 5 — **(A) CONFIRMED**

**Claim as submitted:** *Wicked angels introduced blood sacrifice and incense.*

✅ **Confirmed in substance.** The angels set over mankind transgressed, were captivated by love of women, and begot the demons; they then subdued the human race partly by magic, partly by fear, and partly by teaching men to offer sacrifices, incense and libations — **which they themselves needed** once enslaved by lustful passions.

**Quotation (10 words):** *"teaching them to offer sacrifices, and incense, and libations"*

⚠️ **ONE WORDING PRECISION:** the text says **"sacrifices"**, not *"blood sacrifice."* The submitted phrasing imports a word Justin does not use in this sentence. Minor, but it is the sort of thing an opponent checks.

⛔⛔ **ONE SCOPE CAVEAT, AND IT IS NOT MINOR.** Justin is giving an **aetiology of pagan cult** — explaining where the sacrificial religion of the nations came from, and why the poets ascribed the demons' deeds to the gods. He is not legislating for Christian worship. ⛔ **Extending "demons taught men to burn incense" to "therefore Christians may not use incense ceremonially" is an inference the passage does not make, and it proves far too much: on the same logic the demons taught men to build temples and sing hymns.**

**Register: THEOLOGICAL** — demonic origin. ⚠️ **Rhetorically the strongest-sounding of the eight and evidentially among the weakest for the actual question. Flagged accordingly at §5.**

---

### 6. Epistle of Barnabas ch. 2.5 — **(B) CONFIRMED BUT OVERSTATED**

**Claim as submitted:** *God abolished incense.*

⛔ **Barnabas does not write that sentence, and the verse citation is off by one.** The chapter's own printed heading is *"The Jewish sacrifices are now abolished."* Barnabas's premise, in his own voice, is that God has revealed through all the prophets that He needs **neither sacrifices, nor burnt-offerings, nor oblations** — **incense is not in that list.** The word *incense* occurs exactly once in the chapter and it is **inside a quotation of Isaiah 1**, as one clause in a catalogue that also holds fat of lambs, blood of bulls, fine flour, new moons and sabbaths.

**Quotation (7 words):** *"Incense is a vain abomination unto Me"* — Isaiah's words, quoted by Barnabas.

⛔⛔ **AND THE ABOLITION CLAUSE IS A SEPARATE VERSE WITH A BROADER OBJECT.** The New Advent page carries **no verse numbers at all** — chapter divisions only. Against standard versification, *"Incense is a vain abomination"* falls at **2.5**, but Barnabas's own *"He has therefore abolished these things"* falls at **2.6**, and *"these things"* refers back to the whole Isaianic catalogue. ⛔ **So the citation converts one item in a quoted list into the subject of a sentence Barnabas wrote about something broader.**

**Register: THEOLOGICAL**, and on the richest ground in the set — **divine non-need plus supersession**: the old cult is abolished so that the new law of Christ, *"without the yoke of necessity,"* may have its own oblation. ⭐ **This is the only one of the eight that supplies a fulfilment-of-the-ceremonial-law argument, which is the argument JD's position most wants.**

⚠️⚠️ **BUT NOTE WHAT BARNABAS KEEPS.** His replacement is stated in **sacrificial-odour language**: a broken spirit is the sacrifice, and *"a smell of sweet savour to the Lord is a heart that glorifies Him."* ⛔ **He spiritualises the incense; he does not repudiate the category. A ritualist can point out that Barnabas is comfortable with the imagery — and this is the same symmetry problem the commentary survey records at Keil & Delitzsch and Perowne, arriving from the patristic side.**

### 7. Athenagoras, *Plea (Legatio)* ch. 13 — **(A) CONFIRMED AS CLAIMED — ⭐ THE STRONGEST OF THE EIGHT**

**Claim as submitted:** *God needs no material offerings.*

✅✅ **Confirmed, and it is the best-evidenced citation in the set.** The chapter's own printed heading is *"Why the Christians Do Not Offer Sacrifices"* — so the text is answering exactly the question it is cited for. Athenagoras names **three material offerings and denies all three**: blood, the odour of burnt-offerings, and the fragrance of flowers and incense. **Incense is named explicitly, in the author's own voice, with the cause attached in the same sentence.**

**Quotation (10 words):** *"He is Himself perfect fragrance, needing nothing either within or without"*

**Register: THEOLOGICAL**, unambiguously, resting on **divine self-sufficiency**. The reason is grammatically attached (*"forasmuch as…"*), which is what makes this the cleanest of the eight: there is no reconstruction involved.

⭐⭐ **WHY THIS ONE OUTRANKS THE OTHERS FOR DEPLOYMENT.** Unlike Barnabas it is not a quoted oracle; unlike Lactantius it is not a quoted third party; unlike Tertullian *Apology* 42 it is not undercut two sentences later; and unlike Justin *2 Apology* 5 it is about worship rather than about the origin of paganism. ⚠️ **It remains subject to §3: what Athenagoras denies is that God *needs* material offerings, which a ritualist does not assert.**

⚠️ **The positive half is not an abolition of sacrifice but a spiritualising of it — the noblest sacrifice is to *know* the Framer of all things, and a *"bloodless sacrifice"* is still owed. Recorded because it is the half a hostile reader will quote back.**

---

### 8. Lactantius, *Divine Institutes* 6.25 — **(B) OVERSTATED — ⛔ AND NOT A QUOTATION**

**Claim as submitted:** *Whoever worships God with incense is ignorant of His nature.*

⛔⛔⛔ **THE CHAPTER IS RIGHT AND THE SUBSTANCE IS DEFENSIBLE, BUT THAT SENTENCE IS A CONFLATION OF THREE SEPARATE ONES AND MUST NEVER BE DEPLOYED AS A QUOTATION.** *Divine Institutes* VI.25 is the final chapter of Book VI, printed as *"Of Sacrifice, and of an Offering Worthy of God, and of the Form of Praising God."* Three things are true of it at once:

1. **The word *incense* occurs exactly once in the whole of Book VI**, and it is inside **Lactantius's quotation of Hermes Trismegistus** — Asclepius asks whether incense and other odours should be offered, and Hermes calls the thought the greatest impiety. ⭐ **Lactantius endorses it: *"And he spoke rightly."*** So the substance is his position, by adoption.
2. **The *"ignorant"* clause exists, but its subject is not incense — it is garments and jewels.** Lactantius writes that whoever thinks garments and jewels are valued by God *"is altogether ignorant of what God is."*
3. **The nearest sentence about the *nature* of God and material offerings** speaks of those who *"by no means understand the nature of the Divine Being"* and think a gift is gold, silver, purple or silk, and a sacrifice a victim burnt on an altar.

**Quotation (11 words):** *"a corporeal offering ought not to be presented to an incorporeal being"*

**Register: THEOLOGICAL**, and the most philosophically explicit of the eight — **incorporeality, incorruptibility, self-sufficiency**. God is incorruptible and matter is corruptible; God is unseen and must be worshipped with things unseen. The positive counterpart is stated flatly: *"His offering is innocency of soul; His sacrifice praise and a hymn."*

⚠️⚠️ **TWO CAVEATS THAT LIMIT THIS CITATION'S USE.**

- ⛔ **LACTANTIUS IS NOT ANTE-NICENE IN THE USEFUL SENSE.** He wrote the *Divine Institutes* in the first decade of the fourth century and lived into Constantine's reign. ⚠️ **If the argument is *"the Fathers of the persecuted Church spoke against incense,"* he is the weakest of the eight to lean on — and if the counter-argument is that abstention was a function of the imperial cult, he is the *worst* of the eight, because he is the one writing as that pressure lifted.** ⭐ **That cuts both ways and is worth noticing: a theological objection stated as the persecution ends is harder to explain away as prudence.** The dating is **not** printed on the source page and is supplied from general knowledge; recorded as such.
- ⛔ **The load-bearing incense sentence is a pagan Hermetic author's, adopted by Lactantius.** That is a real endorsement and not nothing — but *"Lactantius says"* is a stronger claim than the text supports, and an opponent who opens the chapter will say so.
