## §0. The answer, first, in one sentence

**Incense IS named in the Reformed Episcopal Church's Prayer Book of 1874 —
four times, every one of them quoted Scripture, and two of them are Malachi
1:11 standing as the second Opening Sentence of Morning Prayer and again of
Evening Prayer, on the first page of the first service of the book.**

There is no rubric about incense, no prohibition of it, and no ceremonial
provision of any kind anywhere in the volume. But the word is in the book, in
a conspicuous place, and **the brief's formulation (d) is defeated by it.**
See §3.

## §1. ITEM 1 — Identifying the book

### VERDICT: **VERIFIED**, with one material edition finding the brief did not anticipate.

### 1(a) Was a distinct REC Prayer Book issued in 1874, and by what authority?

**Yes.** Title page, verbatim:

> THE BOOK OF COMMON PRAYER [of the] Reformed Episcopal Church.
> **ADOPTED, AND SET FORTH FOR USE, BY THE SECOND GENERAL COUNCIL OF THE SAID
> CHURCH, HELD IN THE CITY OF NEW YORK, IN THE MONTH OF MAY, 1874.**
> PHILADELPHIA: James A. Moore, 1220 & 1222 Sansom Street. 1874.

The adopting authority is therefore the **Second General Council**, New York,
May 1874 — not the First General Council of December 1873, which produced the
Declaration of Principles. The Certificate on the following leaf names the
publication committee (B. B. Leacock, Marshall B. Smith, Thomas H. Powers,
Herbert B. Turner; Philadelphia, July 1874) and limits its delegated power to
"corrections in grammar orthography, and punctuation" — i.e. the committee had
no warrant to alter substance. Copyright entered 1874 by Thomas H. Powers.

Working copy: Internet Archive `bookofcommonpray00refo`, Princeton Theological
Seminary Library, 642 leaves, OCR derivative 805,976 bytes,
sha1 `7b9c841239fb0d6b792013a6c41d54e91630a272`.

⚠ **One qualification on adopting authority.** The above is the book's own
testimony about itself — title page and Certificate. It is primary, but it is
not the Council's minutes. **The Journal of the Second General Council was not
consulted** (see §4).

### 1(b) Relation to the 1785 Proposed Book — from the book itself

The brief asks that this be established from the book's own preface or the
Council proceedings, not from secondary description. It can be, three times
over, all inside the volume:

1. The Preface's own footnote: *"The quotations in this Preface are from the
   Preface to the 'Prayer Book of 1785.'"*
2. The Preface's own statement of basis — the reader is directed to compare
   this Book with that of the Church of England, with the present PEC book of
   1789, and with *"the 'Prayer Book of 1785,'* **upon which this revision is
   chiefly based**."
3. **Declaration of Principles III**, printed at p. x of the Prayer Book
   itself: this Church *"accepts the Book of Common Prayer, as it was revised,
   proposed, and recommended for use by the General Convention of the
   Protestant Episcopal Church, A. D. 1785, reserving full liberty to alter,
   abridge, enlarge, and amend the same."*

⚠ **Do not overstate this.** The Preface says **"chiefly based,"** and names
*three* comparators, not one. It does not say the 1874 book reprints the 1785
book, and the common shorthand that the REC "took the 1785 Proposed Book as its
basis" is the REC's own claim of descent plus a reserved liberty to alter —
which it exercised. The Preface also asserts that the PEC "in 1789, retreated
from the position taken by the Convention of 1785." That is the REC's
characterization, printed in its own Prayer Book. Record it as their position;
do not deploy it as an established fact about 1789.

### 1(c) Later editions — and ⚠ THE EDITION FINDING

**The copy searched is not a pure 1874 first issue, and this had to be caught
before anything was concluded from it.**

Bound into the Princeton copy, after the Declaration of Principles and before
the Table of Lessons, is a separately paginated section headed:

> ARTICLES OF RELIGION, **AS ADOPTED BY THE GENERAL COUNCIL OF THE REFORMED
> EPISCOPAL CHURCH, ON THE EIGHTEENTH DAY OF MAY, IN THE YEAR OF OUR LORD ONE
> THOUSAND EIGHT HUNDRED AND SEVENTY-FIVE.**

— with a prefatory extract from the minutes of the **Third** General Council,
Chicago, 18 May 1875. An 1874 title page carrying 1875 matter.

Three internal signs of interpolation: the Contents does not list the Articles
at all; the Articles carry their own separate pagination alongside the front
matter's; and the scanned leaf width changes across the boundary (1727 px
within the Articles, 1686 px in the book proper), consistent with a separately
printed gathering.

**Decisive test against the other 1874 copy.** The University of Illinois copy
(`bookofcommonpray00refo_0`, 612 leaves, "xvi, 588 pages"), same publisher,
same year, same OCLC record, returns **zero** hits for "Supererogation" (the
title and text of Article XVI), zero for "ceremony" (Article XXV), and zero for
"cere" (Article VI). The Princeton copy returns all three. **The Illinois copy
contains no Articles of Religion whatever.** The 30-leaf difference between the
two copies is the Articles gathering.

**Conclusion: the Prayer Book as set forth by the Second General Council in
1874 contained no Articles of Religion.** Everything in this pass that rests on
the Articles is therefore **1875 matter, not 1874 matter**, and is marked as
such throughout. This matters directly to §2: the only two occurrences of the
word "Ceremonies" in the whole volume are both in the Articles, and so are
both outside the 1874 book proper.

Editions worked from, stated plainly: **primary** `bookofcommonpray00refo`
(Princeton, 642 leaves, with 1875 Articles); **corroborating**
`bookofcommonpray00refo_0` (Illinois, 612 leaves, no Articles) and
`commonpray00refo` (Princeton, 842 leaves, with the 1874 Hymns bound in). The
**1932** REC Prayer Book (`bookofcommonpray0000refo`) is lending-restricted and
**could not be searched at all**; nothing here extends to it.

## §2. ITEM 2 — The ceremonial search

### VERDICT: **FALSIFIED** — as to the brief's premise.

The brief's stated reason for this pass was that the Prayer Book's "rubrics are
where a ceremonial prohibition would most naturally sit." **That premise is
wrong, and it is wrong in an interesting way.** The 1874 REC Prayer Book
contains no ceremonial prohibition, because it contains no ceremonial
regulation at all — permissive or prohibitive. The REC did not legislate
ceremonial in its Prayer Book. It removed the subject rather than ruling on it.

The gap recorded at 260835-62 §4(1) is now **closed**.

### 2(a) Is incense named anywhere in the book? — the question the pass exists for

**Yes — four times, and every occurrence is quoted Scripture. Not one is a
rubric, a direction, or a prohibition.**

| Leaf | Passage | Where it sits |
|---|---|---|
| 47 | **Malachi 1:11** | **Second Opening Sentence of MORNING PRAYER** |
| 74 | **Malachi 1:11** | **Opening Sentence of EVENING PRAYER** |
| 407 | Psalm 66:15 | The Psalter |
| 481 | Psalm 141:2 | The Psalter |

The first page of the first service of the book reads, in full:

> **THE ORDER FOR MORNING PRAYER.**
>
> ¶ *The Minister shall begin the Morning Prayer by reading one or more of the
> following Sentences.*
>
> THE Lord is in his holy temple ; let all the earth keep silence before him.
> *Hab. ii, 20.*
>
> From the rising of the sun even unto the going down of the same, my name
> shall be great among the Gentiles; and **in every place incense shall be
> offered unto my name, and a pure offering**: for my name shall be great among
> the heathen, saith the Lord of hosts. *Mal. i, 11.*

The same pair opens Evening Prayer. Psalm 141:2 ("Let my prayer be set forth in
thy sight as the incense") stands in the Psalter, and Psalm 66:15 with it.

This was verified three ways and not taken from a search snippet: read directly
off the running OCR with the office heading and rubric above it; confirmed by
the index placing the Habakkuk and Malachi sentences on the same leaf, and the
identical pairing again at Evening Prayer; and confirmed in **both** other
searchable 1874 scans (leaves 19/46 in the Illinois copy, 23/50 in the other
Princeton copy).

⚠ **Attribution discipline — read before deploying any of this.** What is
established is **placement, and only placement**: the REC printed Malachi 1:11
at the head of both daily offices. The book offers no gloss on the verse and
draws no ceremonial conclusion from it. Any argument about what the REC thereby
conceded or implied is **Analysis** — this project's own — and must be labelled
as such. It is not the REC's statement and must never be relayed as one. The
REC would say, and Article VI as adopted in 1875 gives them the words to say
it, that the Mosaic law "as touching Ceremonies and Rites, does not bind
Christian men."

**A word about the one bound-in hymnal.** The 842-leaf Princeton copy has the
1874 *Hymns recommended for use in the Reformed Episcopal Church* (a separate
Charles W. Quick publication, not part of the Prayer Book) bound in, and two
hymn verses there use incense as metaphor — "As greatful incense to the skies,"
"Perfumed by the holy incense." **These are not Prayer Book text** and should
not be cited as such.

### 2(b) Does any rubric or preface forbid ceremonies generally?

**No. Nothing in the book forbids any ceremony, generally or specifically.**

The only prohibitory matter in the entire volume is **Declaration of Principles
IV**, printed at p. x, which "condemns and rejects" five **doctrines**:
one-polity exclusivism; a sacerdotal priesthood; **"That the Lord's Table is an
altar on which the oblation of the Body and Blood of Christ is offered anew to
the Father"**; presence in the elements; and baptismal regeneration. Every
clause rejects a proposition. None directs anything about furniture, posture,
vesture, or ritual act, and none attaches examples.

That altar clause is, notably, **the only occurrence of the word "altar"
anywhere in the book outside quoted Scripture** — the other eight are
1 Corinthians 9, Joel 2, Matthew 5, and four Psalms.

The nearest thing to a regulating provision is the **Canon on the Use of this
Book of Common Prayer** (Canon 12, Title I), printed at p. xvi immediately
before Morning Prayer. It is reproduced in full in the capture file. It governs
**which book is to be used** and **what words may be inserted**, with a proviso
protecting extempore prayer. **It says nothing whatever about ceremonial,
ornaments, vesture, posture, or ritual acts.** The REC printed a canon inside
its Prayer Book, at the exact point where a reader begins the services, and
used it to regulate text rather than ceremonial.

The one and only ceremonial act named and retained in the whole book is in the
Order of Confirmation, leaf 558: *"we have retained this rite of laying on of
hands upon those who are baptized."*

### 2(c) Is there anything answering to the 1662's "Of Ceremonies"?

**No.** The Contents (given complete in the capture file) lists every section
of the book, and there is no essay, preface-appendix, article or rubric on
ceremonial of any kind. The 1874 book proper does not contain the word
"ceremonies" at all — both occurrences are in the 1875 Articles.

**The comparison the brief asks for is therefore a comparison between a
document and an absence, and that is the finding.** Set against
`src/SRC_PRIMARY_1662_BCP_Of-Ceremonies-Why-Some-Be-Abolished_pdf-pp6-8.txt`:

- **1662 reasons about ceremonies at length and in both directions.** It
  distinguishes ceremonies "worthy to be cut away, and clean rejected" — those
  which "turned to vanity and superstition" and "have much blinded the people,
  and obscured the glory of God" — from those "thought good to reserve them
  still, as well for a decent Order in the Church ... as because they pertain
  to edification." It holds that "the keeping or omitting of a Ceremony, in
  itself considered, is but a small thing," while breach of common order is
  "no small offence," and it reserves the appointment of order to those
  "lawfully called and authorized thereunto."
- **1874 reasons about ceremonies not at all.** It neither abolishes nor
  retains, neither justifies nor condemns. The subject is simply not addressed.

The nearest analogue is a *doctrinal* instrument, not a ceremonial one: the
Declaration's rejected doctrines, and — in the 1875 Articles — the REC's
version of Article XX, which reads *"**A** CHURCH hath power to decree
Ceremonies, and to establish forms of worship"* where the Thirty-Nine Articles
read *"**The** Church hath power to decree Rites or Ceremonies."* Note the
direction of that: it is a **positive grant** of power to decree ceremonies,
limited only by God's written Word. It is not a restraint, and it enumerates
nothing.

### 2(d) The Ornaments Rubric — present, altered, or absent?

**ABSENT. Completely.**

`ornament` — 0 hits. `ornaments` — 0 hits. The hyphen-split fragments `nament`
and `naments` — 0 hits. `vestment` — 0. `vestments` — 0. `vestures` — 0.
`surplice` — 0. `cope` — 0. `chancel` — 0. Confirmed at zero in the Illinois
copy independently.

`vesture` returns five hits, **all quoted Scripture** (Hebrews 1 / Psalm 102,
John 19, Psalm 22) and none referring to clerical dress. `stole` returns one
hit, and it is the verb, in Ephesians 4:28.

The 1874 REC Prayer Book has **no Ornaments Rubric and no replacement for it**.
It does not alter the rubric, does not substitute a Protestant equivalent, and
does not explain its removal. The book simply has nothing where the 1662 has
the Ornaments Rubric.

⚠ **The methodological point that makes this negative worth anything.** The
Internet Archive index matches whole tokens and supports no wildcard, and words
hyphenated across a printed line break are indexed as two separate tokens. This
is not a theoretical worry: **`ceremonies` returns zero on this book even though
the word is plainly present twice**, because the page prints it "Cere-/monies."
Pass 260835-62 recorded this under-reporting as an observed anomaly; this pass
identifies its exact mechanism. Every negative above was therefore run against
both the whole word **and** its plausible hyphen-split fragments, and the
method was validated by confirming that `cere` and `monies` each retrieve the
occurrence that `ceremonies` misses.

The negative rests on three legs: the fragment-compensated index sweep; the
Contents list, read complete, which contains no ceremonial section; and
agreement across **three independent scans** of two different 1874 printings.

## §3. ITEM 3 — What the finding supports

### VERDICT: **PARTIAL.** (b) and (c) stand. **(a) is defeated. (d), as the brief words it, is also defeated** — by this pass's own evidence, and it must not be reworded into survival without saying so.

#### (a) "Incense was among the issues behind the 1873 REC split." — **NOT SUPPORTABLE. DEFEATED.**

Nothing in this pass rescues it, and everything in it deepens the hole.
Incense is absent from all four bodies of text swept at 260835-62 — the
Cummins Memoir (two occurrences in 1,066,765 characters, both outside any
founding document), the Call, the Declaration of Principles, and the 1878
Constitution and Canons (zero) — and it is absent from Cummins's own 1876
itemisation of ritualist abuses, which names altars, candles, vestments,
prostration, auricular confession, prayers for the dead and the mass at
funerals, and no incense.

**And now the Prayer Book cuts against it a second time, harder.** When the REC
sat down to write the liturgy that would embody its protest, it did not
prohibit incense. It printed Malachi 1:11 at the head of Morning and Evening
Prayer. A body for which incense was a live grievance does not open its daily
office, twice, with the verse that is the standard proof-text for it.

#### (b) "Ritualism was among the issues behind the 1873 split." — **SUPPORTABLE. VERIFIED.**

Unaffected by this pass, and independently established at 260835-62 from
Cummins's withdrawal letter of 10 November 1873, which names in its own
numbered reasons "the dangerous errors symbolized by the services customary in
Ritualistic churches" (reason First) and "the whole system of Sacerdotalism and
Ritualism" (reason 2).

⚠ Carry the qualification with it: what Cummins means by "Ritualism" throughout
is **ceremonial as a carrier of sacerdotal doctrine**. He never itemises the
ceremonial acts. The Prayer Book confirms this from the other direction — the
book legislates doctrine and leaves ceremonial alone.

#### (c) "Incense is not named in the REC's founding documents." — **SUPPORTABLE, on the 260835-62 evidence, provided "founding documents" is used strictly.**

Established at 260835-62 across the withdrawal letter, the Call, the First
Council proceedings, the Declaration of Principles, and the 1878 Constitution
and Canons.

⚠ **But this pass narrows the safe scope of the phrase.** If a hearer
understands "founding documents" to include the Church's first Prayer Book —
and many would, since the Prayer Book was adopted by its Second General Council
within six months of the Declaration — then (c) becomes misleading for exactly
the reason (d) is false. **Use (c) only with "founding documents" expressly
defined**, or better, use the formulation at the end of this section, which
does not depend on the phrase at all.

#### (d) "Incense is not named in the REC's founding documents **or its first Prayer Book**." — **FALSE AS WORDED. DEFEATED.**

This is the formulation the pass was commissioned to test, and it fails.

**Incense is named in the REC's first Prayer Book, four times.** Twice it is
Malachi 1:11, standing as an Opening Sentence of Morning Prayer and of Evening
Prayer — not buried in a lectionary table but on the first page of the first
service, printed in a book adopted by a General Council of the Reformed
Episcopal Church.

Anyone who deployed (d) in a live exchange would be handing an informed
opponent a one-move refutation, and the refutation would be devastating in
proportion to how confidently (d) had been asserted. **The brief instructs that
a defeated formulation not be softened into a survivable one. It is not softened
here.** "Named" means named. The verse is there.

The distinction that actually survives is between **naming** and
**regulating** — and it must be stated as a distinction, not smuggled in by
rewording (d) and hoping the difference passes unnoticed.

### The strongest formulation the evidence will carry

> **Ritualism was among the stated causes of the Reformed Episcopal secession
> of 1873. Cummins's letter of withdrawal names "the dangerous errors
> symbolized by the services customary in Ritualistic churches" and "the whole
> system of Sacerdotalism and Ritualism." But incense specifically is named
> nowhere in the REC's founding documents — not in the withdrawal letter, the
> Call, the Declaration of Principles, or the Constitution and Canons — and it
> is absent even from Cummins's own 1876 list of ritualist abuses, which names
> altars, candles, vestments, prostration, auricular confession, prayers for
> the dead and the mass at funerals.**
>
> **Nor did the REC prohibit incense when it had the clearest possible
> opportunity. Its Prayer Book of 1874 contains no ceremonial rubric of any
> kind: no Ornaments Rubric, no equivalent of the 1662's "Of Ceremonies," no
> mention of vestments, candles, or ceremonial in any rubric. Where it
> prohibits, it prohibits doctrines — that the Lord's Table is an altar of
> oblation, and four others — and it attaches no list of ceremonial examples.
> Incense does appear in the book, but only as Scripture: Malachi 1:11 stands
> as an Opening Sentence of both Morning and Evening Prayer, and Psalm 141:2
> in the Psalter.**

**What must NOT be said**, adding to the three already registered at
260835-62 §3(d):

4. **That incense is not named in the REC's first Prayer Book.** It is. Say
   "not named in any rubric," or "named only as Scripture," and say which
   Scripture.
5. **That the REC's Prayer Book permitted incense.** It did not permit it
   either. It is silent on the subject, and silence is the finding. Do not
   convert an absence of prohibition into a permission — that is the same
   error as (a) with the sign reversed.
6. **That printing Malachi 1:11 shows the REC approved of liturgical
   incense.** That is Analysis, and contestable Analysis: the verse stands in
   the 1662, 1789 and 1785 books too, and its presence is inherited, not
   chosen afresh. Its force is that a body allegedly exercised about incense
   left the verse in place at the most conspicuous point in its own revision,
   while removing a great deal else.

### ⭐ The result worth having, flagged and not developed

260835-62 recorded a contrast: the REC, the body that left over ritualism,
wrote a symbolic-doctrinal test into its Constitution and named **no**
ceremonies under it, while the PEC House of Deputies wrote an equivalent test
and named **four**, incense first.

**This pass sharpens that contrast to its strongest form.** The REC's Prayer
Book — the liturgical instrument of the protest, adopted six months after the
Declaration — contains **no ceremonial regulation whatever**, and prints
Malachi 1:11 at the head of both daily offices. In the same year, 1874, the
PEC's House of Deputies passed a clause declaring the use of incense an
instance of ceremonies "setting forth or symbolizing erroneous or doubtful
doctrines," and its House of Bishops struck the list.

So in one year: the seceding Evangelical body declined to name incense at all
and left the Malachi sentence standing; the body it seceded from named it
first of four, and then thought better of it.

Whether and how that is worth anything in the live exchange is **JD's call**.
It is recorded here, not argued, and not drafted.
