## S. Summary verdict

**The 1899 Opinion is fairly and accurately characterised as an English provincial matter with no jurisdictional bearing whatever on the American line — and the American church never responded to it, because on its own constitutional understanding there was nothing addressed to it to respond to.** ⚠️ **But that verdict comes at a price JD needs to see before he uses any of it: in the same period the American Episcopal Church was demonstrably *more* permissive of ritualist ceremonial than the Church of England — it had no ceremonial law at all, it conducted exactly one ritualism proceeding in its whole history, and five years after the Opinion it repealed the one restrictive canon it had. So the Opinion is safe to describe as jurisdictionally inapplicable and *unsafe* to deploy as evidence of what Anglicanism generally held about incense in 1899.**

⭐⭐ **The 1901 negative is the strongest single datum in this section, and it is strong because of what it sits beside.** The Opinion was delivered 31 July 1899. In **1901**, the English and Welsh bishops — every one except Sodor and Man — signed a joint letter binding themselves as a body to *"uphold the duty of submitting to the decision of the Archbishops lately given on questions referred to them, in accordance with the direction in the Book of Common Prayer"* (recorded at `Ritualist_Case_For_Incense_and_the_1899_Opinion.md` §5c, from the 1906 Royal Commission). **In the same year, the American House of Bishops issued its own triennial Pastoral Letter and did not mention the matter at all.** ⛔ **That is not proof of a considered decision to ignore it. It is proof that the Opinion was not among the things the American bishops chose to address their church about, in the first opportunity they had.**

### 1b. What the Opinion says about its own reach

⭐ The Opinion's holding is self-limiting on its face, and the wording matters for §7's drafting guidance. Its operative sentence (quoted in full at `Ritualist_Case_For_Incense_and_the_1899_Opinion.md` §5b) is that the liturgical use of incense *"is not at present enjoined nor permitted by **the law of the Church of England**"* (emphasis added here, not in the original). It construes the **Ornaments Rubric** of the English Prayer Book as an incorporated statute under the **Act of Uniformity 1662**, against the **Act of Uniformity 1559** and **Canon 36 of 1604**. ⛔ **Every one of those four instruments is an Act or Canon of the realm and church of England. None has ever had force in the United States.**

⭐⭐⭐ **AND THE RITUALISTS' OWN JURISDICTIONAL OBJECTION, PRE-REGISTERED IN WRITING BEFORE THE HEARING, WAS PROVINCIAL — WHICH MAKES THE POINT FOR THE AMERICAN LINE A FORTIORI.** Westall's submission opens by stating *"that the only authority which I can recognize as competent finally to determine and settle such matters is the Synod of the Province"* (`Ritualist_Case_For_Incense_and_the_1899_Opinion.md` §5a). ⭐ **If the Opinion did not finally bind a priest of Canterbury province on the ritualists' own theory of authority, it a fortiori binds nobody in a different province — still less in an autonomous national church.** ⚠️ **This is a limb of the ritualist case, not a neutral finding, and is labelled as such; but the Archbishops themselves did not dispute the frame — Temple told the Lords on 16 July 1900 that "the Prayer-book gives me no power to coerce," and both sides in that debate conceded the Opinion bound a clergyman only where his own diocesan took it up.**

### 1c. The one American passage that touches ceremonies at all — reported, and flagged as ambiguous

⚠️⚠️ **The 1904 Pastoral Letter contains one sentence in the neighbourhood of ceremonial, and it is being reported here precisely because a later pass would otherwise find it and think it was missed. ⛔ It is NOT a prohibition, it does NOT name incense, and its grammar is unstable in the transcription used.** In a passage about overworked parish clergy having no time for study, the bishops write:

> "And so the parish priest not unfrequently turns to books, or to ceremonies, which, if they have not the stamp of authority, have, at least, the charm of novelty. Let us not be afraid of either of these--least of all of teaching, which in its effort to reclothe old truths with new vestments, borrows its regalia from the wardrobe of error."

⛔⛔ **THAT SECOND SENTENCE DOES NOT PARSE COHERENTLY AS TRANSCRIBED, AND NO ARGUMENT IS RESTED ON IT.** "Let us not be afraid" followed by "least of all of teaching… borrows its regalia from the wardrobe of error" reads as though a negation has dropped out — the surrounding paragraph is plainly a *warning* about unauthorised teaching, and continues *"he must not eat his mother's bread and yet revile her claim to a divine legitimacy… let him be silent, or withdraw."* ⚠️ **Whether the printed 1904 edition reads as the transcription does was NOT checked against the printed original.** ⭐ **What can safely be said, and all that can: the concern of the passage is doctrinal teaching, not ceremonial, "ceremonies" enters only as a parallel to "books," incense is not named, and no directive of any kind is issued.**

### 1d. What the 1904 letter does say about the English relationship, which is more useful than the ceremonial sentence

⭐⭐ **The Archbishop of Canterbury, Randall Davidson, attended the 1904 General Convention in person, and the Pastoral Letter's opening pages are about that visit.** The framing the American bishops chose is directly on point for the jurisdictional question, and it is fraternal rather than jurisdictional throughout. They describe some of their number as having thought of the Church of England as *"indeed representing a parentage from the loins of which we ourselves had sprung, but more and more as a mother with scant interest in our life and scant concern for our independent history"* — a misapprehension they credit Davidson with dispelling, *"with a fine and high discernment which recognized, all the time, that the Church in Great Britain and the Church in America were parts of one larger whole."*

⛔⛔ **READ THIS CAREFULLY IN BOTH DIRECTIONS, BECAUSE IT CUTS BOTH WAYS AND WILL BE QUOTED BACK.** *"Parts of one larger whole"* is a real communion claim and should not be suppressed — it is the strongest American-side language available for treating English Anglican determinations as having some weight. ⭐ **But "our independent history" is the American bishops' own phrase in the same paragraph, and the entire passage is about affection, recognition and a shared missionary task — there is not one word in it about English law, English courts, archiepiscopal determinations, or any duty of conformity.** ⚠️ **Five years after the Opinion, with the Archbishop of Canterbury standing in front of them, the American bishops did not mention it. At the same Convention they repealed their own ritual canon (§2c).**

## 2. Task 2 — Liturgical incense in the American Episcopal Church around 1899

### 2a. It was practised, publicly and in prominent parishes

Incense was in established use in leading American Anglo-Catholic parishes by 1899, and this is uncontroversial.

- **The Church of the Advent, Boston** — present building constructed 1879–1888, consecrated 1 December 1894 — is described as having incense as a facet of its Anglo-Catholic tradition. ⚠️ **The date at which incense began there was NOT established by this pass; the parish's ritual practices were already a live episcopal dispute in the 1850s (see §3b), which is earlier than any incense date established here.**
- **The Church of St Mary the Virgin, New York** — building completed **1895** — was founded by Thomas McKee Brown to have Catholic ritual in an Episcopal setting, and is nicknamed **"Smoky Mary's"** for the frequency of its incense use. ⚠️ **The nickname's date of origin was NOT established.**

⛔⛔ **NO NUMBERS ARE OFFERED, AND THE ABSENCE IS DELIBERATE. There is no American equivalent of the 1906 Royal Commission's English statistics.** England had a royal commission that counted parishes; the American church never conducted such an inquiry, so *"how widely"* — the brief's own question — **cannot be answered quantitatively from available sources at all.** ⏳ **This is a genuine gap, recorded at §6, not filled by inference from two famous parishes.**

### 2b. The American Prayer Books contain no Ornaments Rubric — verified directly, in the repository's own artifacts

⭐⭐⭐ **THIS IS THE MOST LOAD-BEARING FINDING IN THE FILE, AND IT WAS VERIFIED AGAINST PRIMARY ARTIFACTS RATHER THAN TAKEN FROM COMMENTARY.** The **1899 Opinion's entire ratio decidendi is a construction of the Ornaments Rubric.** The American Prayer Book has no such rubric.

**Test run.** Whole-file text extraction of `src/the-book-of-common-prayer-1928.pdf` (hash `ee7ea56f…` re-verified against `SRC_Manifest.md` this pass):

- `ornament` — **0 occurrences**, case-insensitive, whole book.
- `second year` — **0 occurrences**, whole book.

⛔ **The 1928 American Standard Book contains neither the rubric nor the regnal-year formula on which the Opinion turns.** ⭐ **This is consistent with the well-attested history: the rubric entered the English book in 1559, was retained in 1604 and 1662, and was never adopted by the American church, whose first Prayer Book of 1789 omitted it and put nothing in its place.**

⭐⭐ **And an American High Churchman concedes the consequence in the plainest possible terms.** Louis E. Daniels, writing *for the Anglican Society* and arguing that Americans **should** consider themselves bound by the rubric, is obliged to state the legal position first:

> "The American Church has given us no substitute for this rubric; she has persistently omitted to set forth any ceremonial law, and if this rubric is not our ceremonial law, we have none."

⛔⛔⛔ **NOTE EXACTLY WHAT DANIELS'S ARGUMENT IS, BECAUSE IT IS THE OPPOSITE OF A LEGAL ONE AND MUST NOT BE CITED AS THOUGH IT WERE.** He asks *"does the Ornaments Rubric apply to us of the American Church?"* and answers: **"Again the answer depends upon our loyalty."** ⭐ **His ground is filial loyalty to the mother church plus the American Preface's declaration that "this Church is far from intending to depart from the Church of England in any essential point of doctrine, discipline, or worship" — a moral and interpretive argument, expressly not a jurisdictional one.** ⚠️ **And his own polemical target is the opposite of the Opinion's: he invokes the rubric "chiefly against those who go to excess in their ceremonial," complaining of American parishes where "strange ceremonies are imported from alien sources" — which is itself indirect evidence that American ceremonial ran well beyond English norms by his time.** ⚠️⚠️ **Daniels is UNDATED ("n.d.") in the Project Canterbury record and he describes the Oxford Movement as "of a century ago," which places him loosely in the mid-twentieth century, NOT at 1899. He is used here for the legal-status proposition only, which is not time-sensitive, and for nothing about 1899 practice.**

### 2c. American canon law touching ceremonial — what existed, and what became of it

⭐ Per *An Episcopal Dictionary of the Church*, the American canonical history is this, and it does not resemble England's:

1. **1856** — the ritual dispute at the Church of the Advent, Boston, where **Bishop Manton Eastburn of Massachusetts for many years refused to visit the parish** on account of its ritual practices, produced a canon at that year's General Convention requiring a bishop to visit **every** parish in his jurisdiction at least once every three years. ⭐ **Note the direction of that canon: it constrained the objecting bishop, not the ritualist parish.**
2. **1866** — **the Presiding Bishop of the Episcopal Church, John Henry Hopkins, published *The Law of Ritualism*, urging that a wide variety of ritual uses were canonically permitted in the Episcopal Church**, and predicting that many of the controverted practices would eventually be accepted. ⚠️ **This pass did NOT read *The Law of Ritualism* and does not know what it says about incense specifically. What is established is the Presiding Bishop's general permissive position and the fact of his publishing it.**
   > ⛔⛔ **DATED NOTE, `260835-54` — THIS ITEM IS NOT ALTERED, AND IT IS BOTH UNDER- AND OVER-STATED. The book was located and read this pass. It DOES address incense, at length, and rests it on Malachi 1:11 — see §9.3, which closes §6 gap 6. But "the Presiding Bishop's general permissive position" is a permissive position AS TO LAW ONLY, expressly not as to practice or expediency, and his title page reads "Bishop of Vermont."** ⛔⛔ **AND SEE §9.4: within a year, twenty-eight bishops signed a Declaration censuring "the use of incense" by name.**
3. **1868 and 1871** — canons on ritual were proposed at General Convention and **none was enacted**. ⚠️ **In 1871 resolutions condemning ceremonies expressing doctrines foreign to the church WERE adopted, and the House of Bishops' pastoral letter condemned the new ritualism.** ⛔ **That is a real restrictive act on the American record and it is not being minimised — but it is a resolution and a pastoral letter, not a canon, and it is 28 years before the Opinion.**
   > ⭐⭐ **DATED NOTE, `260835-54` — THIS ITEM IS NOT ALTERED, AND THE 1868 HALF OF IT IS NOW DOCUMENTED FROM THE PRIMARY SOURCE. The 1868 Committee on Ritual reported eleven proposed prohibitions, and "The use of Incense" was item (1), the FIRST of them. Five signatories, named. See §9.2.**
4. **1873** — ritualism was among the issues leading **George David Cummins, Assistant Bishop of Kentucky**, and others into schism to organise the **Reformed Episcopal Church**. ⭐⭐ **Directly relevant to `CL-9`'s chain material: the REC exists in part because the Episcopal Church would NOT restrain ritualism. Its founding is evidence of American permissiveness, from the dissenting side.**
5. **1874** — General Convention **did** pass a canon on ritual. Its test was **doctrinal**: a bishop was to investigate any use of ceremonies or practices in his jurisdiction symbolising **"false or doubtful doctrine,"** and was empowered first to admonish and then to bring to trial any clergyman persisting. ⛔ **The canon "did virtually nothing to slow the expansion of ritual practices."**
   > ⭐⭐ **DATED NOTE, `260835-54` — THIS ITEM IS NOT ALTERED, AND THE DRAFTING HISTORY BEHIND IT IS NOW REGISTERED FROM THE PRIMARY SOURCE, BECAUSE IT CHANGES WHAT THE ENACTED CANON MEANS. The Deputies' DRAFT named incense as forbidden example (a). The ENACTED canon omits incense and the crucifix and lists only elevation, acts of adoration, and "all other like acts." See §9.2 and — for what that omission does and does not mean — §9.1.**
6. **1877** — **exactly one trial for ritualism ever took place.** Oliver Prescott received **episcopal admonishment** for his ritual practices. ⛔ **That is the entire American disciplinary record for ritualism.**
7. **1904** — **the canon on ritual was quietly repealed at that year's General Convention.**

⭐⭐⭐ **AND HERE IS THE DIFFERENCE THAT MATTERS MOST FOR ANY COMPARISON WITH 1899, BECAUSE IT IS A DIFFERENCE OF TEST AND NOT MERELY OF SEVERITY. The 1874 American canon asked whether a ceremony symbolised false or doubtful DOCTRINE. The 1899 English Opinion asked whether a ceremony was ENJOINED OR PERMITTED BY LAW.**    > ⛔⛔⛔ **DATED NOTE, `260835-54` — THE SENTENCE THAT FOLLOWS IS NOT ALTERED AND IS THE ONE PLACE IN THIS FILE THAT OVERSTATES IN JD'S FAVOUR. "Incense passes the American test comfortably" is an inference, and the man best placed to know denied its premise from the floor six days later: the House did not decide incense passes the doctrinal test, it REFUSED TO DECLARE THAT INCENSE SYMBOLIZES FALSE DOCTRINE, and DeKoven said in terms that the canon "leaves that just where it was before." ⛔ The refusal is narrower than the pass, and §9.1 registers the difference.**

⛔⛔ **Incense passes the American test comfortably and fails the English one — and it fails the English one on grounds that have no American analogue, since the Opinion's reasoning runs entirely through the Ornaments Rubric, the Acts of Uniformity and Canon 36.** ⭐ **This is exactly the argument James DeKoven made at the 1871 General Convention: that ritual practices such as altar candles, incense and genuflection do not symbolise transubstantiation, that they predate that doctrine, and that they are shared by Orthodox and Lutheran churches which deny it.** ⚠️ **DeKoven lost the canonical vote in 1874 and his defence of ritualism cost him the bishopric of Illinois; the Dictionary's own judgement is that "DeKoven's vision of comprehensiveness in worship ultimately prevailed in the Episcopal Church."**

## 3. Task 3 — Comparative disposition: more permissive, more restrictive, or comparable?

### 3a. The answer

⭐⭐⭐ **MORE PERMISSIVE, AND BY A WIDE MARGIN, ON EVERY AXIS THIS PASS COULD TEST. ⚠️ THIS IS THE FINDING THAT RUNS AGAINST JD'S POSITION AND IT IS STATED WITHOUT HEDGING.**

| Axis | Church of England, c. 1899 | Protestant Episcopal Church, c. 1899 |
|---|---|---|
| **Ceremonial law** | Ornaments Rubric, statutory via the Act of Uniformity 1662; Canon 36 of 1604; the 1559 Act's *"none other or otherwise"* | ⛔ **None.** No Ornaments Rubric, no ceremonial law of any kind, on its own High Churchmen's admission |
| **Courts with jurisdiction over ritual** | Court of Arches; Judicial Committee of the Privy Council; the Public Worship Regulation Act 1874's machinery | ⛔ **None comparable.** Diocesan ecclesiastical courts only, under a canon whose test was doctrinal |
| **Restrictive canon in force** | Yes, and statutory | 1874 ritual canon, **repealed 1904** |
| **Enforcement record** | Ritualist prosecutions; clergy imprisoned under the PWRA; *Martin v. Mackonochie*, *Hebbert v. Purchas*, *Read v. Bishop of Lincoln* | ⛔ **One admonishment, ever** (Prescott, 1877). **No trial concluded against a ritualist** |
| **Metropolitical determination on incense** | The 1899 Opinion, followed by the 1901 joint episcopal letter binding the bench | ⛔ **None found** |
| **Direction of travel, 1899–1904** | Tightening: Opinion 1899, episcopal letter 1901, Royal Commission 1904–06 | ⭐ **Loosening: ritual canon repealed 1904** |

## 5. Task 5 — Assessment

### 5a. The verdict, in two parts, because the question has two parts

**On jurisdictional reach — the Opinion is an English provincial matter, and the case is strong.**

1. It was **not a court** and carried **no coercive power**, by Archbishop Temple's own statement in the Lords (`…§5c`).
2. Its holding is **expressly framed as the law "of the Church of England."**
3. Its **reasoning runs wholly through instruments with no American force** — the Ornaments Rubric, the Acts of Uniformity 1559 and 1662, Canon 36 of 1604.
4. The **American Prayer Book contains no Ornaments Rubric**, verified directly in the 1928 Standard Book, so the Opinion's subject matter has no American counterpart to construe.
5. The **1662 itself disclaims prescribing ceremonies to other nations** (§3c).
6. **PECUSA had been autonomous since 1789**, and its bishops in 1901 and 1904 said nothing about the Opinion in either Pastoral Letter, read in full.
7. Even in England its force was **moral and episcopal, mediated through each diocesan** — and no American diocesan was found taking it up.

**On evidential weight as a statement of Anglican practice — the Opinion is weak, and JD should not use it that way.**

⛔⛔⛔ **THIS IS THE HALF THAT RUNS AGAINST THE POSITION THE BRIEF ANTICIPATED DEFENDING, AND IT IS THE HALF THAT MATTERS MORE PRACTICALLY.** In 1899 the American church had **no ceremonial law**, had conducted **one ritualism proceeding in its history** ending in admonishment, had a Presiding Bishop on record since 1866 arguing that a wide range of ritual uses were canonically permitted, had incense established in prominent parishes, and **repealed its only restrictive canon in 1904**. ⭐ **An instrument that a substantial and growing part of the communion neither adopted nor discussed, and whose reasoning could not have applied there, is not evidence of what "Anglicanism" held.**

> ⛔⛔ **DATED NOTE, `260835-54` — THE PARAGRAPH ABOVE IS NOT ALTERED, AND ITS CONCLUSION STANDS, BUT ONE OF ITS FIVE SUPPORTING CLAUSES IS NOW KNOWN TO BE DOING LESS WORK THAN IT APPEARS TO.** ⚠️ ***"had a Presiding Bishop on record since 1866 arguing that a wide range of ritual uses were canonically permitted"* is TRUE AS WRITTEN and was verified this pass** (§9.3) — ⭐ **and on incense specifically it is stronger than 260835-53 could claim, since Hopkins argues incense's lawfulness at length and rests it on Malachi 1:11.** ⛔⛔ **But it must never be advanced without the two facts beside it, both of which an informed interlocutor will supply if JD does not: Hopkins argues LAWFULNESS and expressly declines to recommend the practice, to adopt it, or to say whether it is beneficial — *"whether expedient or not, can never be justly considered unlawful"* is his own formula; and within a year twenty-eight of his brother bishops signed a Declaration censuring incense by name** (§9.4). ⛔ **A Presiding Bishop writing about ritual LAW, publicly repudiated on the point by two-thirds of his own bench, is a materially weaker datum for American permissiveness than the clause reads as. The paragraph's conclusion does not depend on it — the four other clauses carry it — but the clause alone will not.**

### 5b. ⭐⭐⭐ The strongest counter-argument to my own verdict, stated at full strength

⭐⭐ **It is not the jurisdictional one, because the jurisdictional one loses. It is this: jurisdiction is the wrong question if the appeal is to the 1662.**

**The argument, put as its best advocate would put it.** The 1899 Opinion is the most authoritative construction ever given, by the two metropolitans of the Church of England sitting under the Prayer Book's own reference provision, of **the ceremonial law of the 1662 Book of Common Prayer** — after a full adversarial hearing at which the ritualists deployed their entire evidentiary and legal case through counsel and nine expert appendices, and lost on the merits. ⭐ **So the moment anyone appeals to the 1662 as his standard — rather than to the 1928, or to the American canons, or to a bare claim of catholic liberty — the Opinion becomes directly relevant to him, not as law binding his jurisdiction, but as the best available authoritative reading of the very book he has named.** ⭐ **Autonomy answers "must he obey?" It does not answer "what does the book he appeals to actually provide?"** ⚠️ **And Daniels supplies the American-side version of this in a High Church voice: the American church "has persistently omitted to set forth any ceremonial law," so an American who wants a ceremonial standard at all has, on Daniels's account, nowhere to look but the English rubric — which is exactly the instrument the Opinion construes.**

**Why I still hold the verdict, while granting real force to that.**

- ⭐ **The 1662 itself blocks the extension** (§3c): it prescribes nothing but to its own people and commends to every country such ceremonies as it thinks best. ⛔ **An appeal to the 1662 therefore does not import English ceremonial law wholesale — it imports a book that expressly declines to legislate for other countries.**
- ⭐ **The Opinion's reasoning is statutory, not liturgical.** Its decisive move — the inversion argument, that an ornament is subordinate to a ceremony and has no place where no ceremony is prescribed — is deployed to construe the **Elizabethan statute**, and its supporting authorities are Canon 36 and the 1559 Act's *"none other or otherwise."* ⛔ **Strip out the statutes and the Opinion is not self-standing.**
- ⭐ **The Opinion twice declines to say what an evidential use of it would need it to say.** It states that *"the Church has never spoken of incense as an evil thing"*; that *"we are far from saying that incense in itself is an unsuitable or undesirable accompaniment to Divine worship"*; and that *"even now the liturgical use of incense is not by law permanently excluded from the Church's Ritual."* ⛔⛔ **It is a ruling about English positive law at a date, expressly not a judgement about incense.** ⭐ **Anyone deploying it as the latter is deploying it against its own terms — and that is true whichever jurisdiction the hearer stands in.**

⚠️ **Where the counter-argument genuinely wins, and this is conceded rather than argued around: if the question on the table is "what does the 1662's ceremonial provision authorise?", the 1899 Opinion is the leading authority and cannot be waved away as foreign. The jurisdictional point does not touch that question at all.**

## 7. ⭐⭐⭐ What can and cannot be safely claimed in conversation

### 7a. Wording that will hold up

⭐ **These will survive a well-informed challenge. The formulations are deliberate; the italicised words are load-bearing.**

- *"The 1899 Opinion was given by the Archbishops of Canterbury and York on a reference from the Bishop of London under the English Prayer Book's own provision. Its holding is that liturgical incense is not enjoined or permitted by the law of the Church of England — that is the Opinion's own phrase for its own scope."*
- *"Its reasoning is a construction of the Ornaments Rubric against the Acts of Uniformity of 1559 and 1662 and Canon 36 of 1604. The American Prayer Book has never contained the Ornaments Rubric — the 1928 Standard Book contains neither the rubric nor the regnal-year formula the whole argument turns on."*
- *"American High Churchmen who wanted the rubric to apply in America conceded that it did so only as a matter of loyalty to the mother church, not of law — and granted in the same breath that the American church 'has persistently omitted to set forth any ceremonial law.'"*
- *"Even in England the Opinion was not a court judgement and carried no coercive force; Archbishop Temple said so himself in the Lords, and it bound a clergyman only where his own diocesan took it up."*
- *"The 1662's own section on ceremonies says that in these things the Church of England condemns no other nations and prescribes nothing but to its own people, and that every country should use such ceremonies as it thinks best."*
- *"So far as I can find, the American House of Bishops never addressed the Opinion. Their 1901 and 1904 Pastoral Letters — the first two after it — don't mention it, and the Archbishop of Canterbury was at the 1904 Convention in person."*

### 9.1 ⛔⛔⛔ TASK 1b — THE CORRECTION: THE BISHOPS' STRIKING OF INCENSE WAS NOT AN AFFIRMATIVE PERMISSION

⭐⭐⭐ **THIS IS THE CORRECTION THE PASS EXISTS TO MAKE, AND IT NARROWS A CLAIM THIS FILE AND THE WIDER PROJECT HAVE BEEN LEANING ON.**

**The framing being corrected.** That the 1874 canon's final text omits incense has been read — here at §2c and in the orchestration thread's own evidential-weight reasoning — as the American bishops *permitting* incense, or as incense *passing* the American doctrinal test. ⛔ **It is neither.**

**What DeKoven said, on the floor, immediately after final passage, 31 October 1874, verbatim:**

> "In the next place, there are two specifications left out, which said that the use of incense and the use of the crucifix symbolized false doctrine. I hope the reverend gentlemen from Pennsylvania will believe it when I say that **nothing in this Canon authorizes the use of incense or the use of the crucifix. It leaves that just where it was before. If it was lawful before, it is lawful now; if it was unlawful before, it is still unlawful.**"

⭐⭐ **The man who had led the fight against the incense clause five days earlier, and won it, told the House from the floor that his victory conferred nothing.** ⛔ **The striking was a refusal to declare that incense SYMBOLIZES FALSE DOCTRINE. It was not a declaration that incense is lawful, and DeKoven expressly denied that it was.**

⛔⛔ **WHY THE DIFFERENCE MATTERS, PUT PLAINLY.** A refusal to condemn leaves the prior legal position untouched, whatever that position was. An affirmative permission would have settled it. ⭐ **The narrower claim is still useful and still runs JD's way — the American church, asked point-blank in 1874 to declare incense symbolic of false doctrine, declined — but it is a claim about what the House would not say, not about what it authorised.**

⚠️ **CROSS-REFERENCE, AS THE BRIEF REQUIRED — WHERE `260835-53` OVERSTATES.** Two places, both now carrying dated notes: **§2c's closing** (*"Incense passes the American test comfortably"* — an inference, and the premise is denied by DeKoven himself) and **§7b's third bullet** (*"never the subject of a canon naming it"* — true of enacted canons only). ⭐ **§5a's evidential-weight paragraph does NOT overstate on this point and is left standing; its defect is a different one, at §9.3.**

### 9.4 ⛔⛔⛔ THE "TWENTY-FOUR BISHOPS' DECLARATION" — RESOLVED, AND IT IS THE WORST SINGLE DATUM IN THIS FILE FOR JD'S POSITION AND ALSO SUPPLIES ITS BEST

### 9.6 ⭐⭐⭐ TASK 3 — AMERICAN INCENSE CHRONOLOGY, AND THE HONEST ANSWER IS THAT USE WAS RARE AND LATE

⭐⭐ **THE ANCHOR DATUM, VERBATIM FROM THE FLOOR, 26 OCTOBER 1874 — AND IT IS FROM THE ONE MAN IN THE COUNTRY BEST PLACED TO KNOW:**

> "I am a Presbyter of this Church of twenty years' standing, and I am accused of Ritualism. **(b) Was there an American promotional literature? ⛔ ON THE EVIDENCE FOUND, ESSENTIALLY NOT IN THE PERIOD — AND THE ASYMMETRY WITH ENGLAND IS THE FINDING.**

- **England:** *Directorium Anglicanum* (1858), Walker's *The Ritual Reason Why* (1866, enlarged 1868), *Ritual Notes* (1st ed. 1894), the English Church Union's output, and finally a 173-page book of expert testimony in 1899.
- **America:** ⛔ **the first substantial ceremonial manual is McGarvey and Burnett, *The Ceremonies of the Mass* (New York/London: Longmans, Green, 1905), prepared under the Clerical Union for the Maintenance and Defense of Catholic Principles — 1905, not the 1860s–90s.** The American edition of Walker's *Ritual Reason Why* is **Milwaukee, 1908**, forty years after the English original, with no earlier American printing found. The American Missal project begins 1911 and the *American Missal* is **1931**. The one genuine American incense tract found — *Tracts on Principles of Divine Worship, No. 7: Incense*, Men's Guild, St Ignatius' Church, New York — is **undated and internally post-1900**.
- ⚠️ **A negative that is bounded, not absolute:** *The Book of Other Rites and Ceremonies of the Church* (Milwaukee, 1893), the nearest thing to an episcopally-commended American supplementary ceremonial of the period, contains **no reference to incense at all** — ⛔ **but its scope is occasional offices, not the Mass, so it bears only lightly.**
- ⚠️ **Percival's *The Theological Value of Incense* appears to have been written FOR the 1899 English case** — the printing carries no headnote of prior publication and no incense title appears in his bibliographies. ⛔ **That is an argument from silence and is reported as one** (§6 gap 15). ⭐ **A correction to the brief's own framing, made rather than passed over: the "Second Treatise" in that appendix is by Darwell Stone, English, Principal of Dorchester Missionary College — this file's §2e already has this right.**

Article-level search needs HathiTrust or ProQuest, which were not reachable. ⚠️ Their plausibility as venues is NOT evidence that they carried such articles, and this negative is worth very little** (§6 gap 14).

**(c) ⭐⭐⭐ THE HONEST BOTTOM LINE, GIVEN AS THE BRIEF DEMANDED IT BE GIVEN EITHER WAY.**

⛔⛔ **AMERICAN EPISCOPAL INCENSE USE WAS RARE AND LATE. That is a finding, not a failure, and it is registered as one.** No dated attestation earlier than Christmas 1877 surfaced. The Advent's ritual war of the 1840s–50s was not about incense. St Clement's was not using it at its 1879 trial. St Stephen's Providence first used it in 1894 and says so. Twenty-eight bishops in 1866 called it among "usages that have never been known." The country's only self-described clerical ritualist had never seen it used in an American church in 1874. **And the American debate was therefore substantially PREEMPTIVE — three attempts in eight years to prohibit a practice that was barely present.**

⚠️⚠️ **THREE CAUTIONS AGAINST OVER-READING THIS, AND THEY ARE NOT DECORATIVE.** ⛔ **(1) Almost every positive date above is secondary — twentieth-century parish histories written from records not seen, or third-hand. Only the NEGATIVE evidence rests on documents read directly: the 1874 debate, the 1879 charges, the 1845–56 Eastburn correspondence, and the 1866 Declaration.** ⛔ **(2) Contemporary newspaper full text — Chronicling America, the *NYT* archive — is exactly where a pre-1877 attestation would surface, since the 1860s–70s press covered ritualist services as scandal and in physical detail, and that layer was NOT searched.** ⛔ **(3) St Alban's, New York (1865) is a blank, and it is the parish the scholarly literature calls the first avowedly ritualistic church in the United States.** ⭐ **Christmas 1877 is therefore "the earliest date these sources will support," NOT a demonstrated first, and must be stated that way.**

### 9.7 ⛔⛔⛔ TASK 4 — THE THING THAT CUTS AGAINST JD, REGISTERED PLAINLY, BOTH HALVES

⭐⭐⭐ **DeKoven's floor argument against the incense clause on 26 October 1874 rested on Psalm 141:2 and Malachi 1:11, quoted from the floor. ⛔ AND HE EXPRESSLY DECLINED TO ARGUE THAT MALACHI 1:11 WAS A PROPHECY OF SOMETHING LITERALLY TO TAKE PLACE, ATTRIBUTED THAT VIEW TO "RITUALISTS" WITH EVIDENT DISTANCE, AND RESTED ON SYMBOLIC MEANING ONLY.**

**Psalm 141:2, verbatim:**

> "For this House to forbid the use of incense is a very proper thing, perhaps; but for this House to say that the use of incense symbolizes false doctrines, is for this House to put itself in utter and total opposition to the Holy Scriptures; for, remember, what does David say? 'Let my prayer be set forth in Thy sight as the incense, and let the lifting up of my hands be an evening sacrifice.' In other words, David says that the use of incense, to which that holy prophet and king was accustomed—having not lived in our own day—**symbolized prayer**; and will this church say—is it prepared to say,—that the use of incense, which symbolizes prayer, symbolizes false doctrine?"

**Malachi 1:11, verbatim — and this is the half that cuts:**

> "Then again, I heard it read in St. Thomas' Church yesterday morning at the beginning of the services, 'From the rising of the sun even unto the going down of the same, my name shall be great among the Gentiles, and in every place incense shall be offered unto my name, and a pure offering; for my name shall be great among the heathen, saith the Lord of Hosts.' **I am not going to enter into the question whether that was a prophecy of something that was literally to take place. Some people say it was, but I am afraid they are Ritualists. My only question is as to its symbolical meaning.** The prophet Malachi holds that incense symbolizes the pure offering,—**I suppose the Eucharistic offering**; and for the sake of this argument I am willing for the moment to concede that that offering is nothing but an offering of prayer and thanksgiving; **though I do not think so.**"

⛔⛔ **HALF ONE — AND IT IS THE ONE JD MUST SEE. America's foremost ritualist, defending incense at the top of his form, in the highest-stakes setting available to him, WOULD NOT MAKE THE LITERAL-FULFILMENT ARGUMENT. He set it aside in a sentence, and the distance is audible: "Some people say it was, but I am afraid they are Ritualists" — said by a self-described Ritualist, about Ritualists, to put space between himself and the reading.** ⚠️ **He was arguing to a hostile House and had tactical reason not to press it. ⛔ That is a possible explanation and it is NOT evidence; he did not say it, and nothing here may be attributed to a motive he did not state.** ⭐⭐ **And the tactical explanation is weakened by §9.3: Hopkins, a Presiding Bishop writing eight years earlier in a book rather than a floor fight, DID take Malachi 1:11 as express prophecy of literal Gentile incense. The literal reading was available in American print, from the highest office in the church, and DeKoven still declined it.**

⭐⭐ **HALF TWO — AND IT IS REGISTERED WITH EQUAL WEIGHT, AS THE BRIEF REQUIRED. He DID read the pure offering as the Eucharistic offering** ("The prophet Malachi holds that incense symbolizes the pure offering,—I suppose the Eucharistic offering… **though I do not think so**," the last clause refusing the concession he had just offered for argument's sake) — **and he DID use an Aaron-with-the-censer typology, at length:**

> "Or again—and this is something more awful—when Aaron stood between the dead and the living with the censer in his hands, and the smoke of the incense was wafted to heaven; the people were saved. What did he typify but that Eternal Son of God who alone stands between the dead and the living, and whose mediation for the souls of men forever ascends to the right hand of God? and what did the ascending incense symbolize but the atoning Sacrifice and the everlasting Mediation?"

⭐ **He extends the same move to the Day of Atonement: the priest before the mercy-seat, "and clouds of incense covered it, typifying the ceaseless intercession of the Son of God."**

⭐⭐ **BOTH HALVES STAND TOGETHER AND NEITHER MAY BE DEPLOYED WITHOUT THE OTHER. The Eucharistic reading and the typology are adjacent to Rev. James's position and are available to it. The refusal of literal fulfilment is adjacent to JD's and is available to him. ⛔ Quoting either half alone misrepresents the passage, and the passage is short enough that an interlocutor can read the rest in thirty seconds.**

⛔⛔ **AND THE STRUCTURAL POINT, WHICH IS THE SHARPEST THING IN THIS SECTION: DeKoven's ENTIRE argument is that incense does not symbolize FALSE doctrine. It is not an argument that incense is commanded, prophesied, or required. ⭐ That is why §9.1's correction matters — the House gave him exactly what he asked for and no more.**

### 9.8 ⭐ REPLACEMENT WORDING FOR §7b's THIRD BULLET

⛔ **§7b's bullet is not altered. This is what should be said instead of it, and every clause is sourced above:**

> *"Anglicans of that period didn't hold incense to be unlawful everywhere — in 1866 the American Presiding Bishop published a book arguing it was lawful in the American church precisely because that church had never prohibited it, and no American canon ever named it. But I'd put the American side honestly: twenty-eight bishops censured incense by name the next year, a convention committee put it first on a prohibition list in 1868, and the 1874 draft canon named it before the clause was struck. What the American church never did was legislate against it — and the likeliest reason is that there was almost nothing to legislate against. The earliest American use anyone can date is Christmas 1877."*

⚠️ **The last sentence is the one that will be tested; §9.6(c)'s three cautions are the answer, and conceding them early is what makes the rest credible.**
